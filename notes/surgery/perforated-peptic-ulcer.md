# Perforated peptic ulcer

> Free perforation of a gastric or duodenal ulcer into the peritoneal cavity, causing sudden chemical then bacterial peritonitis. It is the commonest cause of pneumoperitoneum and a classic surgical emergency; NEET PG asks the site, the erect chest X-ray, the investigation of choice and the omental patch.

## High-yield
- The **commonest site** of peptic ulcer perforation is the **anterior wall of the first part of the duodenum** [1].
- **Anterior** duodenal ulcers tend to **perforate**; **posterior** duodenal ulcers tend to **bleed** (erosion of the **gastroduodenal artery**) [1].
- Perforated peptic ulcer is the **most common cause of pneumoperitoneum** (free intraperitoneal gas) [1].
- Onset is **sudden, severe epigastric pain** that rapidly becomes generalised, with **board-like rigidity** and a patient who **lies still** [1].
- **Erect chest X-ray** is the first investigation and shows **free gas under the diaphragm** in roughly three-quarters of cases [2].
- **CT abdomen** is the most sensitive investigation for free gas and the **investigation of choice** when the X-ray is equivocal [1].
- Absence of free gas on X-ray does **not exclude** perforation [1].
- Standard operation is **closure with an omental patch** (Graham / Cellan-Jones repair) plus **peritoneal lavage**, open or laparoscopic [1].
- After repair, **H. pylori eradication** and a **PPI** prevent recurrence; routine definitive acid-reducing surgery is no longer needed [1].

## Definition
- A peptic ulcer that erodes through the full thickness of the stomach or duodenal wall into the **free peritoneal cavity** [1].
- A perforation that is walled off by adjacent organs (omentum, liver, gallbladder) is a **sealed (contained) perforation** [1].

## Causes / Risk factors
- **NSAIDs** (including low-dose aspirin) are a major cause, especially in the **elderly** [1].
- ***Helicobacter pylori*** infection is the other main cause of the underlying ulcer [1].
- **Smoking** is a risk factor for perforation [1].
- **Corticosteroids**, particularly combined with NSAIDs, add risk and can **mask the physical signs** of peritonitis [1].
- **Crack cocaine** use is associated with perforation in younger patients [1].
- Physiological stress (burns, head injury, critical illness) causes stress ulcers that may perforate; **Curling's ulcer** follows **burns** and **Cushing's ulcer** follows **head injury / raised intracranial pressure** [1].

## Pathophysiology
- Gastroduodenal contents (acid, bile, enzymes) entering the peritoneum cause an initial **chemical peritonitis** [1].
- Bacterial contamination follows over hours, converting it to **bacterial (suppurative) peritonitis** and sepsis [1].
- Classic clinical course has three stages: **(1) prostration/chemical peritonitis**, **(2) reaction** (an apparent improvement as peritoneal exudate dilutes the irritant), **(3) diffuse bacterial peritonitis** [1].
- The "period of reaction" can mislead the clinician into thinking the patient is improving [1].
- Gastric contents may track down the **right paracolic gutter** to the right iliac fossa, mimicking **acute appendicitis** (**Valentino's syndrome**) [1].
- Gas escaping through the perforation rises to lie beneath the diaphragm on standing, producing **pneumoperitoneum** [1].

## Clinical features
- **Sudden-onset, severe epigastric pain**, often with a precise remembered time of onset, spreading over the whole abdomen [1].
- There may be a preceding history of dyspepsia or NSAID use, though many patients have **no prior ulcer history** [1].
- **Shoulder-tip pain** from diaphragmatic irritation (referred via the **phrenic nerve, C3–C5**) [1].
- The patient **lies still** because movement worsens peritoneal pain (contrast with the restless patient of colic) [1].
- **Board-like (generalised) rigidity** with marked guarding and tenderness [1].
- **Obliteration (loss) of liver dullness** on percussion from free gas [1].
- **Absent or reduced bowel sounds** as ileus develops [1].
- Tachycardia, hypotension and fever appear as bacterial peritonitis and sepsis develop [1].
- Signs may be **blunted in the elderly, immunosuppressed or those on steroids** [1].

## Investigations
- **Erect chest X-ray**: free gas under the diaphragm (**pneumoperitoneum**), best seen under the **right hemidiaphragm** [1].
- The patient should sit upright for about **10 minutes** before the erect film to let gas rise [1].
- An erect chest X-ray can detect as little as about **1–2 mL** of free gas [2] ⚠ Unverified.
- If the patient cannot sit up, a **left lateral decubitus** abdominal film shows gas between the liver and the lateral abdominal wall [1].
- Signs of pneumoperitoneum on a supine film include **Rigler's sign** (both sides of the bowel wall visible), the **football sign** and the **falciform ligament sign** [1].
- **Chilaiditi sign** (colon interposed between liver and diaphragm) is a **pseudo-pneumoperitoneum** and must not be mistaken for free gas [1].
- **CT abdomen** is the most sensitive test, detects small volumes of free gas and fluid, and often localises the site [1].
- A **water-soluble contrast (Gastrografin) study** can show whether a perforation has sealed, when conservative management is considered [1].
- **Serum amylase** may be mildly raised in perforation, so it should be measured to help separate it from acute pancreatitis (where it is usually markedly raised) [1].
- Baseline bloods (FBC, urea/electrolytes, lactate, group and save, blood gas) guide resuscitation [1].
- Barium is **contraindicated** when perforation is suspected because barium peritonitis is severe [1].

## Management
- **Resuscitation first**: IV access and crystalloid fluids, **nasogastric tube** aspiration, urinary catheter for output monitoring, analgesia [1].
- **IV broad-spectrum antibiotics** and an **IV proton pump inhibitor** are started on diagnosis [1].
- **Surgery is the standard treatment** for free perforation with peritonitis [1].
- The perforation is closed with an **omental patch**: the **Graham patch** uses a free omental plug and the **Cellan-Jones repair** uses a **pedicled tongue of omentum** sutured over the defect [1].
- In exam usage the pedicled omental patch closure is commonly called **"Graham's patch"** [2].
- **Thorough peritoneal lavage** with warm saline is done at the end of repair [1].
- **Laparoscopic repair** is an accepted alternative to open repair in suitable, stable patients [1].
- A **gastric ulcer perforation** should be **biopsied or excised** because it may be a **perforated gastric carcinoma** [1].
- **Conservative (non-operative) management** — nasogastric suction, IV fluids, antibiotics, PPI (the **Taylor method**) — is reserved for selected stable patients with a **sealed perforation** confirmed on contrast study [1].
- Conservative management is unsuitable for the elderly and for those not improving within about 12–24 hours, who need surgery [1] ⚠ Unverified.
- Postoperatively, test for and **eradicate *H. pylori***, stop NSAIDs where possible and continue a PPI [1].
- **Definitive acid-reducing surgery** (vagotomy) at the time of perforation is **no longer routine** in the PPI and *H. pylori* era [1].
- Large ("giant") perforations, commonly described as larger than about 2 cm, may not be closable by a patch and may need **partial gastrectomy** or other techniques [1] ⚠ Unverified.
- **Boey score** predicts mortality using three factors: **shock on admission** (systolic BP <100 mmHg), **major co-existing medical illness**, and **delay to surgery over 24 hours** [1].
- Mortality rises with **age, delay to surgery, shock and comorbidity** [1].

## Complications
- **Residual intra-abdominal abscess**, especially **subphrenic** and **pelvic** abscess [1].
- **Leak** from the repair [1].
- **Wound infection** and **wound dehiscence** [1].
- **Septic shock** and **multiorgan failure** [1].
- Recurrent ulceration if *H. pylori* is not eradicated or NSAIDs continue [1].
- Postoperative chest infection [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- PYQs have expected "Graham's patch" as the operation for perforated duodenal ulcer; strictly, Graham described a **free omental graft** and the **pedicled** omental patch is the **Cellan-Jones** repair, but either name scores as "omental patch closure" [2].
- PYQs have expected **erect chest X-ray** as the first / initial investigation and sometimes as the "investigation of choice"; current practice treats **CT abdomen** as the most sensitive investigation and the test of choice when doubt remains [2].
- PYQs often quote free gas under the diaphragm in about **70%** of perforations; texts vary, so treat the figure as approximate and remember a normal film does **not exclude** perforation [2].
- PYQs have offered **vagotomy with drainage** or **partial gastrectomy** as options; current practice is simple omental patch closure with *H. pylori* eradication and PPI, not definitive acid-reducing surgery [2].
- Shoulder-tip pain here is from diaphragmatic irritation; **Kehr's sign** (left shoulder pain) is the eponym tied to **splenic rupture**, a common distractor [2].
- A **perforated gastric ulcer** must be biopsied because the exam's trap answer is to treat it like a duodenal ulcer and miss **carcinoma** [2].
