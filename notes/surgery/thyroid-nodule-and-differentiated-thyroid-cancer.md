# Thyroid nodule and differentiated thyroid cancer

> A thyroid nodule is common, and most are benign; the work-up (TSH, ultrasound, FNAC with Bethesda reporting) exists to find the small fraction that are cancer [1]. Differentiated thyroid cancer (papillary and follicular) arises from follicular cells, takes up iodine, makes thyroglobulin and has an excellent prognosis, so it is treated with surgery, selective radioiodine and TSH suppression [1]. NEET PG tests the nodule work-up, the papillary-versus-follicular contrasts, and the follow-up marker [2].

## High-yield
- **Papillary carcinoma** is the **most common** thyroid cancer, about 80–85% of cases [1].
- **FNAC** (ultrasound-guided) is the **investigation of choice** for the nature of a thyroid nodule [1].
- The first tests for a nodule are **serum TSH** and **neck ultrasound**; a radionuclide scan is done only if TSH is low [1].
- Papillary carcinoma is diagnosed by its **nuclear features** (Orphan Annie eye/ground-glass nuclei, nuclear grooves, intranuclear pseudoinclusions) and often shows **psammoma bodies** [1].
- Follicular carcinoma is diagnosed only by **capsular or vascular invasion** on histology, so FNAC cannot separate follicular adenoma from carcinoma [1].
- Papillary carcinoma spreads by **lymphatics** to neck nodes; follicular carcinoma spreads by **blood** to **lungs and bone** [1].
- **Childhood head-and-neck radiation** is the main known risk factor, and it predisposes chiefly to **papillary** carcinoma [1].
- After total thyroidectomy (± radioiodine), **serum thyroglobulin** is the tumour marker for recurrence [1].
- **BRAF V600E** is the most common mutation in papillary carcinoma; **RAS** mutations and **PAX8–PPARγ** fusion characterise follicular tumours [1].
- **Transient hypocalcaemia** from parathyroid hypofunction is the most common complication after total thyroidectomy [1].

## Definition
- A **thyroid nodule** is a discrete lesion within the thyroid that is radiologically distinct from the surrounding parenchyma [1].
- **Differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC)** is cancer of the thyroid follicular cell that retains differentiated function: **papillary**, **follicular** and **oncocytic (Hürthle cell)** carcinoma [1].
- Medullary carcinoma (from parafollicular C cells) and anaplastic carcinoma are **not** differentiated thyroid cancers [1].
- **Papillary microcarcinoma** is papillary carcinoma **≤1 cm** in size [1].
- **NIFTP** (non-invasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary-like nuclear features), formerly non-invasive encapsulated follicular variant of PTC, was reclassified in 2016 as a non-malignant neoplasm [1].

## Causes / Risk factors
### Thyroid nodule
- Thyroid nodules are palpable in about **4–7%** of adults but are found by ultrasound in up to about half of adults [1].
- About **5–15%** of thyroid nodules are malignant [1].
- Nodules are more common in **women**, with age, and in **iodine deficiency** [1].
- Features that raise the suspicion of malignancy in a nodule are **male sex**, **age <20 or >60 years**, **hard or fixed** nodule, **rapid growth**, **hoarseness** (recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement), **cervical lymphadenopathy**, history of **neck irradiation** and **family history** of thyroid cancer [1].
- A **solitary nodule** is more likely to be malignant than a nodule within a multinodular goitre [1].

### Differentiated thyroid cancer
- **Ionising radiation** to the neck in childhood (including the **Chernobyl** exposure) increases the risk of **papillary** carcinoma [1].
- **Iodine deficiency** is associated with **follicular** carcinoma, while iodine-sufficient regions have a higher proportion of **papillary** carcinoma [1].
- **Hashimoto thyroiditis** is the main risk factor for **primary thyroid lymphoma** [1].
- Familial syndromes with non-medullary thyroid cancer include **familial adenomatous polyposis** (cribriform-morular variant of papillary carcinoma), **Cowden syndrome** (follicular carcinoma) and **Carney complex** [1].
- DTC is about **three times more common in women** [1].

## Pathophysiology
### Papillary carcinoma (PTC)
- PTC forms **papillae** with fibrovascular cores, but the diagnosis rests on the **nuclear features**, not the papillary architecture [1].
- Characteristic nuclei are **optically clear ("Orphan Annie eye", ground-glass)**, with **nuclear grooves** and **intranuclear pseudoinclusions** [1].
- **Psammoma bodies** (concentric laminated calcifications) are characteristic of PTC [1].
- PTC is often **multifocal** within the gland [1].
- PTC spreads mainly via **lymphatics** to the central and lateral neck nodes [1].
- The so-called **"lateral aberrant thyroid"** is a cervical lymph-node metastasis from papillary carcinoma [1].
- The **follicular variant** of PTC has follicular architecture but papillary nuclei and is classified as papillary carcinoma [1].
- **Tall cell**, **columnar cell**, **hobnail** and **diffuse sclerosing** variants are more aggressive forms of PTC [1].
- **BRAF V600E** point mutation is the most common genetic alteration in PTC [1].
- **RET/PTC rearrangements** occur in PTC, especially radiation-associated and childhood tumours [1].

### Follicular carcinoma (FTC)
- FTC is a follicular-cell carcinoma **without** the nuclear features of PTC [1].
- The diagnosis of FTC requires **capsular invasion** and/or **vascular invasion**; cytology alone cannot make it [1].
- FTC is classified as **minimally invasive** (capsular invasion only), **encapsulated angioinvasive** or **widely invasive** [1].
- FTC spreads mainly **haematogenously**, to **lung** and **bone** [1].
- Bone metastases from FTC are typically **osteolytic** and may be **pulsatile** [1].
- **RAS** mutations and **PAX8–PPARγ** rearrangement are characteristic of follicular neoplasms [1].
- Lymph-node metastasis is **uncommon** in FTC compared with PTC [1].

### Oncocytic (Hürthle cell) carcinoma
- Hürthle cell carcinoma is made of **oncocytic cells** rich in **mitochondria** with granular eosinophilic cytoplasm [1].
- It was traditionally a **variant of follicular carcinoma** but is classified as a separate entity, **oncocytic carcinoma**, in the WHO 2022 classification [1].
- Oncocytic carcinoma **takes up radioiodine poorly** compared with FTC [1].

## Clinical features
- Most DTCs present as a **painless solitary thyroid nodule** in a euthyroid patient [1].
- PTC may present with an **enlarged cervical lymph node** with or without a palpable thyroid primary [1].
- FTC may first present with **distant metastasis**, such as a pathological fracture or lung deposits [1].
- **Hoarseness** from recurrent laryngeal nerve invasion, **dysphagia**, **stridor** and a **fixed hard mass** suggest locally advanced disease [1].
- PTC is the most common thyroid cancer in **children** and has the best prognosis of all thyroid cancers [1].
- PTC is the most common malignancy arising in a **thyroglossal duct cyst** [1].
- Patients with DTC are usually **euthyroid** [1].

## Investigations
### Thyroid nodule work-up
- The first step is **serum TSH** and **ultrasound** of the thyroid and neck [1].
- If **TSH is low**, a **radionuclide (radioiodine or technetium) scan** is done to look for a **hyperfunctioning ("hot") nodule** [1].
- A **hot nodule** is rarely malignant and usually does not need FNAC [1].
- A **cold nodule** carries a higher risk of malignancy than a hot nodule, but most cold nodules are still benign [1].
- Ultrasound features suspicious for malignancy are **hypoechogenicity**, **microcalcifications**, **taller-than-wide** shape, **irregular or infiltrative margins**, **extrathyroidal extension** and **suspicious cervical nodes** [1].
- A **purely cystic** nodule has a very low malignancy risk [1].
- Ultrasound risk is graded by systems such as **ACR TI-RADS** (TR1–TR5) and the **ATA sonographic patterns**, which set the size threshold for FNAC [1].
- By ATA guidance, a **high-suspicion** nodule is sampled at **≥1 cm**, a **low-suspicion** nodule at **≥1.5 cm**, and a **very-low-suspicion** nodule at **≥2 cm** or observed [1].
- **Ultrasound-guided FNAC** is the investigation of choice to determine the nature of a nodule [1].
- FNAC is reported in the **Bethesda System**, six categories: **I** non-diagnostic, **II** benign, **III** atypia of undetermined significance (AUS), **IV** follicular neoplasm, **V** suspicious for malignancy, **VI** malignant [1].
- Bethesda **II** carries the lowest and Bethesda **VI** the highest risk of malignancy [1].
- **FNAC cannot distinguish follicular adenoma from follicular carcinoma**, because invasion is a histological feature; such lesions are reported as Bethesda IV [1].
- **Frozen section** is of little value for follicular lesions for the same reason [1].
- **Molecular testing** (e.g. BRAF, RAS, gene-expression classifiers) can refine the risk of Bethesda III and IV nodules [1].
- **CT or MRI** is used for **retrosternal**, fixed or locally invasive disease; **iodinated contrast** delays radioiodine treatment by weeks [1].
- Routine **serum thyroglobulin** is **not** useful for diagnosing a nodule pre-operatively [1].
- **Serum calcitonin** is raised in **medullary** carcinoma, not in DTC [1].

### Staging and risk
- In **AJCC 8th edition**, the age cut-off for DTC staging is **55 years** (it was 45 in the 7th edition) [1].
- In DTC patients **younger than 55**, there are only two stages: **stage I** without distant metastasis and **stage II** with distant metastasis [1].
- Prognostic scoring systems for DTC include **AMES**, **AGES**, **MACIS** and **GAMES** [1].
- Adverse prognostic factors are **older age**, **male sex**, **large tumour size**, **extrathyroidal extension**, **distant metastasis**, **aggressive histology** and **incomplete resection** [1].
- The **ATA risk-stratification** system classifies DTC as **low, intermediate or high risk of recurrence** to guide radioiodine and TSH suppression [1].

## Management
### Nodule by Bethesda category
- **Bethesda I**: repeat FNAC under ultrasound guidance [1].
- **Bethesda II**: clinical and ultrasound follow-up; surgery only for compressive symptoms or cosmesis [1].
- **Bethesda III**: repeat FNAC, molecular testing, or diagnostic lobectomy [1].
- **Bethesda IV**: diagnostic **hemithyroidectomy (lobectomy)** or molecular testing [1].
- **Bethesda V and VI**: surgery as for thyroid cancer [1].
- A **toxic (hot) nodule** is treated with **radioiodine** or **lobectomy** after control of thyrotoxicosis [1].

### Surgery for DTC
- **Total thyroidectomy** is indicated for tumours **>4 cm**, gross **extrathyroidal extension**, **nodal or distant metastasis**, **bilateral disease** or prior **neck irradiation** [1].
- For low-risk unifocal intrathyroidal DTC of **1–4 cm** without nodes, **lobectomy or total thyroidectomy** are both acceptable under ATA 2015 guidance [1].
- For low-risk **papillary microcarcinoma (<1 cm)** without nodes or extension, **lobectomy** or **active surveillance** is acceptable [1].
- If a lobectomy specimen shows carcinoma needing radioiodine, **completion thyroidectomy** is done [1].
- **Therapeutic central (level VI) neck dissection** is done for clinically involved central nodes [1].
- **Lateral (modified radical) neck dissection** is done for **biopsy-proven lateral nodal metastasis**, preserving the sternocleidomastoid, internal jugular vein and accessory nerve [1].
- **Prophylactic lateral neck dissection** is **not** done in DTC [1].
- **"Berry picking"** (removing only the enlarged node) is not recommended because of higher recurrence [1].

### Radioiodine (I-131)
- Radioiodine is used for **remnant ablation**, **adjuvant** treatment and treatment of **iodine-avid metastatic disease** after total thyroidectomy [1].
- Radioiodine is **not routinely** given for **ATA low-risk** DTC [1].
- Radioiodine is recommended for **ATA high-risk** disease, including distant metastasis and gross extrathyroidal extension [1].
- Radioiodine uptake needs a **high TSH (>30 mU/L)**, achieved by **thyroid hormone withdrawal** or **recombinant human TSH**, with a **low-iodine diet** beforehand [1].
- A **30 mCi (1.1 GBq)** dose is adequate for **low-risk remnant ablation** [1].
- Radioiodine is **contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding** [1].
- Side effects of radioiodine include **sialadenitis**, **xerostomia**, taste change and, rarely, second malignancies [1].
- **Medullary** carcinoma does not take up iodine, so radioiodine has **no role** in it [1].

### TSH suppression and follow-up
- **Levothyroxine** is given after surgery to replace thyroid hormone and to **suppress TSH**, because TSH stimulates DTC growth [1].
- The degree of TSH suppression is matched to risk: **below 0.1 mU/L for high-risk** disease and **low-normal TSH for low-risk** disease [1].
- **Serum thyroglobulin** is the tumour marker after total thyroidectomy and radioiodine; a **rising or detectable** level suggests recurrence [1].
- **Anti-thyroglobulin antibodies** must be measured with thyroglobulin because they make the thyroglobulin assay unreliable [1].
- **Neck ultrasound** is the main imaging for detecting locoregional recurrence [1].
- A **diagnostic whole-body radioiodine scan** detects iodine-avid residual or metastatic disease [1].
- **FDG-PET** is useful when thyroglobulin is raised but the radioiodine scan is negative (**radioiodine-refractory** disease) [1].
- **Tyrosine kinase inhibitors** such as **sorafenib** and **lenvatinib** are used for progressive **radioiodine-refractory** DTC [1].

### Prognosis
- DTC has an excellent prognosis, with **10-year survival above 90%** for papillary carcinoma [1].
- Follicular carcinoma has a **slightly worse** prognosis than papillary carcinoma, mainly because of distant metastasis [1].

## Complications
### Of thyroidectomy
- **Hypocalcaemia** from transient **hypoparathyroidism** is the most common complication after total thyroidectomy [1].
- Hypocalcaemia presents with **perioral tingling**, **Chvostek** and **Trousseau** signs and tetany, usually within the first 24–72 hours [1].
- **Unilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) palsy** causes **hoarseness** [1].
- **Bilateral RLN palsy** can cause **stridor** and airway obstruction needing reintubation or **tracheostomy** [1].
- Injury to the **external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve** causes loss of **high-pitched voice** and **voice fatigue**, from paralysis of the **cricothyroid** muscle [1].
- **Post-thyroidectomy haemorrhage** with a neck haematoma can compress the airway and is treated by **opening the wound at the bedside** to evacuate the clot [1].
- The RLN is at greatest risk near the **ligament of Berry** and the **inferior thyroid artery**, and the **tubercle of Zuckerkandl** is a landmark for it [1].
- **Thyroid storm** is a risk if a thyrotoxic patient is operated on without being made euthyroid [1].

### Of the disease
- Locally advanced DTC may invade the **trachea**, **oesophagus**, **larynx** and **RLN** [1].
- A small proportion of DTCs **dedifferentiate** into poorly differentiated or anaplastic carcinoma [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- PYQs have expected **FNAC** as "the first investigation" for a solitary thyroid nodule; current guideline practice is **TSH and ultrasound first**, with FNAC as the investigation of choice for the nature of the nodule [2].
- PYQs have expected **total thyroidectomy** for all DTC; current ATA guidance accepts **lobectomy** for low-risk tumours of 1–4 cm and lobectomy or active surveillance for low-risk microcarcinoma [2].
- PYQs have expected an age cut-off of **45 years** in DTC staging; the current AJCC 8th edition uses **55 years** [2].
- PYQs and older texts list **Hürthle cell carcinoma as a variant of follicular carcinoma**; WHO 2022 classifies it separately as **oncocytic carcinoma** [2].
- PYQs have treated the **encapsulated follicular variant of PTC** as cancer; since 2016 the non-invasive form is called **NIFTP** and is not a carcinoma [2].
- Exam keys pair **psammoma bodies with PTC**; they also occur in serous ovarian tumours, meningioma and mesothelioma, which may appear as distractors [2].
