# Acute appendicitis

> Acute inflammation of the vermiform appendix, usually from obstruction of its lumen; the commonest acute surgical emergency of the abdomen. NEET PG tests the anatomy (positions, blood supply), the named signs, the Alvarado score, imaging choices and the management of appendicular mass and abscess.

## High-yield
- Acute appendicitis is the **most common surgical cause of acute abdomen** (most common abdominal surgical emergency) [1].
- **Luminal obstruction** is the initiating event: **lymphoid hyperplasia** is the commonest cause in children and young people, a **faecolith (appendicolith)** in adults [1].
- The commonest position of the appendix is **retrocaecal**; the second commonest is **pelvic** [1].
- The **appendicular artery** (branch of the **ileocolic artery**) is an **end artery**, so thrombosis causes gangrene and perforation [1].
- Pain begins **periumbilical** (visceral, T10) and **shifts to the right iliac fossa** when the parietal peritoneum is involved [1].
- **Murphy's triad / sequence**: pain first, then vomiting, then fever; vomiting before pain argues against appendicitis [1].
- **Alvarado (MANTRELS) score** is out of **10**; tenderness in RIF and leucocytosis score **2** each, every other item scores 1 [1].
- **Ultrasound** is the first imaging test in children and pregnant women; **contrast-enhanced CT** is the most accurate test in adults [1].
- Treatment of choice for acute appendicitis is **appendicectomy**, now usually **laparoscopic** [1].
- **Appendicular mass** is treated conservatively by the **Ochsner–Sherren regimen**; an **appendicular abscess** is drained (usually percutaneously, image-guided) [1].

## Definition
- Acute appendicitis is acute inflammation of the vermiform appendix [1].
- It is classified as **uncomplicated** (inflamed, no gangrene or perforation) or **complicated** (gangrene, perforation, abscess, mass or peritonitis) [1].

## Causes / Risk factors
- **Obstruction of the lumen** is the usual initiating cause [1].
  - **Lymphoid hyperplasia** (often after viral infection) predominates in children and adolescents [1].
  - **Faecolith / appendicolith** predominates in adults; its presence is associated with a higher risk of perforation [1].
  - Less common causes: foreign bodies, worms (e.g. *Enterobius vermicularis*, *Ascaris*), strictures, and tumours such as **carcinoid** or caecal carcinoma [1].
- Peak incidence is in the **second and third decades** [1].
- A low-fibre (Western) diet is associated with higher incidence [1].

### Surgical anatomy
- The base of the appendix lies where the **three taeniae coli converge** on the caecum; following the taeniae locates the appendix at operation [1].
- The surface marking of the base is **McBurney's point**: junction of the **lateral one-third and medial two-thirds** of the line from the **anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus** [1].
- Positions, commonest first: **retrocaecal**, then **pelvic**; subcaecal, pre-ileal and post-ileal positions are less common [1].
- The **appendicular artery**, a branch of the **ileocolic artery**, runs in the **mesoappendix** and is an **end artery** [1].
- The appendix is rich in **lymphoid tissue**, which is maximal in young people and atrophies with age [1].

## Pathophysiology
- Obstruction → continued mucus secretion → rising intraluminal pressure → venous congestion and bacterial overgrowth → mucosal ulceration → transmural inflammation [1].
- Rising pressure then compromises arterial supply; because the appendicular artery is an end artery, **gangrene** follows, usually first at the **antimesenteric border**, and then **perforation** [1].
- Early distension stimulates **visceral afferents entering at T10**, so pain is referred to the **periumbilical** region [1].
- When inflammation reaches the **parietal peritoneum**, pain becomes **somatic and localised to the right iliac fossa** [1].
- The **greater omentum** and loops of bowel may wall off the inflamed appendix, forming an **appendicular mass**; pus within it forms an **appendicular abscess** [1].
- Perforation is more frequent at the **extremes of age** (young children and the elderly); in young children the omentum is short and poorly developed, so walling-off is less effective [1].

## Clinical features
### Symptoms
- **Periumbilical colicky pain shifting to the RIF** (migratory pain) is the classic symptom [1].
- **Anorexia** is almost constant; nausea and a few episodes of vomiting follow the onset of pain [1].
- **Low-grade fever** is usual; a high fever suggests perforation or another diagnosis [1].
- **Retrocaecal** appendicitis may give flank or loin pain with little anterior tenderness and little guarding [1].
- **Pelvic** appendicitis may cause **diarrhoea**, urinary **frequency** or dysuria (irritation of rectum or bladder), with suprapubic pain [1].

### Signs
- **Tenderness at McBurney's point** with guarding in the RIF [1].
- **Rebound tenderness** (Blumberg's sign) indicates peritoneal irritation [1].
- **Rovsing's sign**: pressure in the **left iliac fossa** causes pain in the **right iliac fossa** [1].
- **Psoas sign**: pain on **extension of the right hip**; suggests a **retrocaecal** appendix lying on the psoas [1].
- **Obturator sign**: pain on **internal rotation of the flexed right hip**; suggests a **pelvic** appendix in contact with obturator internus [1].
- **Dunphy's sign**: increased RIF pain on coughing [1].
- **Hyperaesthesia in Sherren's triangle** (bounded by the ASIS, umbilicus and pubic symphysis) may be present [1].
- **Tenderness on rectal examination** on the right side suggests a **pelvic** appendix [1].
- **Pointing sign**: the patient points to the site of maximal pain in the RIF [1].
- Pregnancy: the appendix is displaced **upward and laterally** by the gravid uterus, so pain and tenderness may be higher than McBurney's point [1].
- **Alder's sign** (pregnancy): tenderness that stays fixed when the patient turns onto the left side suggests appendicular rather than uterine origin [1] ⚠ Unverified.

### Scoring systems
- **Alvarado (MANTRELS) score**, total **10** [1]:

| Item | Points |
|---|---|
| **M**igration of pain to RIF | 1 |
| **A**norexia | 1 |
| **N**ausea / vomiting | 1 |
| **T**enderness in RIF | 2 |
| **R**ebound tenderness | 1 |
| **E**levated temperature (≥ 37.3 °C) | 1 |
| **L**eucocytosis | 2 |
| **S**hift to the left (neutrophilia) | 1 |

- A score of **7 or more** makes appendicitis likely; a low score (≤ 4) makes it unlikely [1].
- The **Appendicitis Inflammatory Response (AIR) score** adds CRP and is used in current guidelines for risk stratification [1].
- The **Pediatric Appendicitis Score (PAS)** is used in children [1].

## Investigations
- Diagnosis is primarily **clinical**, supported by scores and imaging [1].
- **Total leucocyte count**: leucocytosis with neutrophilia; **CRP** raised, especially in complicated disease [1].
- **Urine analysis** to exclude urinary tract infection or stone; a few red or white cells may occur when an inflamed appendix lies against the ureter or bladder [1].
- **Urine / serum β-hCG** in every woman of reproductive age to exclude **ectopic pregnancy** [1].
- **Ultrasound** (graded compression): **non-compressible, blind-ending tubular structure > 6 mm** in diameter; first-line in **children** and **pregnancy** [1].
- **Contrast-enhanced CT** abdomen has the highest sensitivity and specificity in adults and shows complications (abscess, perforation, appendicolith) [1].
- **MRI** (without gadolinium) is used in pregnancy when ultrasound is inconclusive [1].
- Plain X-ray abdomen has little role; it may show an **appendicolith** or a sentinel loop [1].

### Differential diagnosis
- Children: **mesenteric lymphadenitis**, gastroenteritis, **Meckel's diverticulitis**, intussusception, right lower lobe pneumonia [1].
- Women: **ectopic pregnancy**, ruptured or twisted ovarian cyst, **pelvic inflammatory disease**, mittelschmerz (ruptured Graafian follicle) [1].
- Adults: ureteric colic, pyelonephritis, perforated peptic ulcer, Crohn's disease (terminal ileitis), caecal diverticulitis [1].
- Elderly: **caecal carcinoma**, sigmoid diverticulitis, mesenteric ischaemia [1].

## Management
### Acute (uncomplicated and complicated) appendicitis
- Initial care: nil by mouth, IV fluids, analgesia (analgesia does not mask the diagnosis and should not be withheld), and IV antibiotics [1].
- Treatment of choice: **appendicectomy**; **laparoscopic appendicectomy** is preferred where available (less wound infection, less pain, faster recovery, and allows diagnostic laparoscopy, useful in women of reproductive age and the obese) [1].
- **Open appendicectomy** incisions [1]:
  - **Gridiron (McBurney's) incision**: oblique, through McBurney's point, perpendicular to the spinoumbilical line; muscles are split along their fibres [1].
  - **Lanz incision**: transverse, in the skin crease, cosmetically better [1].
  - **Rutherford Morison incision**: extension of the gridiron incision upward and laterally (muscle-cutting) for a difficult retrocaecal appendix or more access [1].
- At operation the appendix is found by tracing the **taeniae coli** to their convergence on the caecum [1].
- If the appendix is normal, look for another cause, including **Meckel's diverticulum** by inspecting about **60 cm (2 feet)** of terminal ileum [1].
- Antibiotics: a single prophylactic dose for uncomplicated appendicitis; a short postoperative course (about 3–5 days) for complicated appendicitis [1].
- **Antibiotics-first (non-operative) treatment** is an accepted option for selected adults with **CT-proven uncomplicated** appendicitis; a significant proportion later need appendicectomy for recurrence, and an **appendicolith** predicts failure [1].
- Pregnancy: appendicectomy (laparoscopic is acceptable) should not be delayed, because perforation greatly increases **fetal loss** [1].

### Appendicular mass
- An appendicular mass (lump in RIF usually after 3–5 days of symptoms) is managed conservatively by the **Ochsner–Sherren regimen**: nil by mouth/liquids, IV fluids, IV antibiotics, analgesia, and charting of pulse, temperature and the size of the mass [1].
- The regimen is stopped and surgery done if there is rising pulse, increasing pain or mass size, spreading peritonitis or vomiting [1].
- **Interval appendicectomy** was traditionally done after **6–8 weeks**; it is now selective, but **colonoscopy or CT is advised in older patients (over about 40)** to exclude **caecal carcinoma** [1].

### Appendicular abscess
- Treated by **image-guided percutaneous drainage** with antibiotics; open drainage if this fails or is not available [1].
- A **pelvic abscess** may drain spontaneously or be drained **through the rectum** (or vagina) [1].

## Complications
### Of the disease
- **Perforation** with **generalised peritonitis** [1].
- **Appendicular mass** and **appendicular abscess** (including **pelvic abscess** and subphrenic abscess) [1].
- **Pylephlebitis (portal pyaemia)**: septic thrombophlebitis of the portal venous system, with rigors, jaundice and **liver abscesses** [1].
- Intestinal obstruction from adhesions or abscess [1].

### Of appendicectomy
- **Wound infection** is the most common complication after appendicectomy [1].
- **Residual intra-abdominal / pelvic abscess** [1].
- **Faecal fistula** (from slipped stump ligature, caecal necrosis, or an unrecognised underlying cause such as Crohn's disease, carcinoma or tuberculosis) [1].
- **Right inguinal hernia** from injury to the **ilioinguinal nerve** with a gridiron incision [1].
- **Stump appendicitis** from leaving a long stump [1].
- Adhesive intestinal obstruction later [1].
- Female infertility (tubal damage) after perforated appendicitis in girls [1] ⚠ Unverified.

### Incidental tumours
- **Carcinoid (neuroendocrine tumour)** is the commonest tumour of the appendix, usually at the **tip**; tumours **< 2 cm** are usually cured by appendicectomy, while larger ones or base involvement need **right hemicolectomy** [1].
- **Mucocele** of the appendix may rupture and cause **pseudomyxoma peritonei** [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- **Rovsing vs psoas vs obturator** is a favourite trap: Rovsing = LIF pressure, RIF pain; psoas = hip extension, retrocaecal; obturator = internal rotation of flexed hip, pelvic [1].
- **Alvarado weighting**: only **tenderness in RIF** and **leucocytosis** score 2; the trap option makes migration or rebound score 2 [1].
- PYQs have expected **ultrasound** as the investigation of choice for appendicitis in general; current practice uses ultrasound first in children and pregnancy and **CECT** as the most accurate test in adults [2].
- PYQs have expected **interval appendicectomy at 6–8 weeks** after an appendicular mass; current practice makes routine interval appendicectomy selective, with colonoscopy/CT in older patients to exclude malignancy [2].
- PYQs have expected **appendicectomy is the only treatment**; current practice accepts **antibiotics-first** for selected CT-confirmed uncomplicated appendicitis in adults [2].
- PYQs have expected **carcinoid > 2 cm → right hemicolectomy**, and **< 2 cm at the tip → appendicectomy alone** [2].
- Some keys give the **pelvic** position as commonest because it is commonest at operation in some series; the standard-text answer is **retrocaecal** [2] ⚠ Unverified.
- **Post-ileal** position is sometimes keyed as the one with the most atypical presentation and delayed diagnosis [2] ⚠ Unverified.
