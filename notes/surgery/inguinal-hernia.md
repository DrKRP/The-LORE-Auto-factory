# Inguinal hernia

> Protrusion of abdominal contents through the inguinal canal region, either through the deep ring (indirect) or through the weak posterior wall in Hesselbach's triangle (direct). It is the commonest abdominal wall hernia, and NEET PG repeatedly tests canal anatomy, direct vs indirect distinction, clinical tests, named hernias and the choice of repair.

## High-yield
- **Inguinal hernia** is the most common type of abdominal wall hernia in both men and women [1].
- **Indirect** inguinal hernia is the most common type of inguinal hernia overall, in both sexes, and in children [1].
- An indirect hernia passes through the **deep ring, lateral** to the inferior epigastric vessels; a direct hernia bulges through **Hesselbach's triangle, medial** to them [1].
- **Hesselbach's triangle** is bounded by the **inferior epigastric artery** laterally, the **lateral border of the rectus sheath** (rectus abdominis) medially and the **inguinal ligament** inferiorly [1].
- The deep ring lies about **1.25 cm above the midinguinal point** [1].
- An inguinal hernia emerges **above and medial to the pubic tubercle**; a femoral hernia lies **below and lateral** to it [1].
- In a **sliding hernia** a viscus (sigmoid colon on the left, caecum on the right, or bladder) forms part of the **wall of the sac** [1].
- **Lichtenstein tension-free mesh repair** is the standard open repair for adult inguinal hernia [1].
- In children the operation is **herniotomy** alone (high ligation and excision of the sac), without repair of the posterior wall [1].
- **Laparoscopic repair (TEP/TAPP)** is preferred for **bilateral** hernias and for **recurrence after an open repair** [1].

## Definition
- A hernia is the protrusion of a viscus or part of a viscus through an abnormal opening in the wall of the cavity that contains it [1].
- **Indirect (oblique)** inguinal hernia: the sac enters the inguinal canal through the deep ring, lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, and travels along the canal within the spermatic cord coverings [1].
- **Direct** inguinal hernia: the sac bulges forwards through the posterior wall of the canal (transversalis fascia) in Hesselbach's triangle, medial to the inferior epigastric vessels [1].
- **Pantaloon (saddle-bag) hernia**: coexisting direct and indirect sacs that straddle the inferior epigastric vessels [1].
- **Herniotomy** is excision of the sac; **herniorrhaphy** is herniotomy plus repair of the posterior wall with the patient's own tissue; **hernioplasty** is reinforcement of the posterior wall with a prosthetic mesh [1].

### Relevant anatomy of the inguinal canal
- The inguinal canal is an oblique passage about **4 cm** long in the adult, running from the deep ring to the superficial ring, above and parallel to the medial half of the inguinal ligament [1].
- The **deep ring** is an opening in the **transversalis fascia**; the **superficial ring** is a triangular opening in the **external oblique aponeurosis**, above and lateral to the pubic tubercle [1].
- **Anterior wall**: external oblique aponeurosis throughout, reinforced laterally by the internal oblique [1].
- **Posterior wall**: transversalis fascia throughout, reinforced medially by the **conjoint tendon** [1].
- **Roof**: arching fibres of the internal oblique and transversus abdominis [1].
- **Floor**: the inguinal ligament, with the lacunar ligament medially [1].
- Contents in the male are the **spermatic cord**, the **ilioinguinal nerve** and the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve; in the female the cord is replaced by the **round ligament of the uterus** [1].
- The ilioinguinal nerve enters the canal through its wall, not through the deep ring, and leaves through the superficial ring [1].
- Coverings of the cord: **external spermatic fascia** from the external oblique, **cremasteric fascia and muscle** from the internal oblique, and **internal spermatic fascia** from the transversalis fascia [1].

## Causes / Risk factors
- An indirect hernia is congenital in origin, arising in a **patent processus vaginalis** [1].
- A direct hernia is acquired, from **weakness of the posterior wall** (transversalis fascia) [1].
- Inguinal hernia is far more common in **males** than in females [1].
- Risk factors for hernia include conditions that raise intra-abdominal pressure (**chronic cough, constipation, straining at micturition from prostatic enlargement**, heavy lifting, ascites, pregnancy) [1].
- **Smoking** and connective tissue (collagen) disorders weaken the abdominal wall and predispose to hernia, especially direct and recurrent hernia [1].
- Previous lower abdominal incisions such as an appendicectomy wound can damage the ilioinguinal nerve and weaken the canal [1].
- In children, inguinal hernia is more common in **boys**, in **preterm** infants and on the **right side** [1].
- The right-sided predominance in children is attributed to later descent of the right testis and later closure of the right processus vaginalis [1].

## Pathophysiology
- The processus vaginalis normally obliterates after testicular descent; persistence of the whole process gives a congenital hernia reaching the testis, while partial persistence gives a funicular hernia stopping above the testis [1].
- In a direct hernia the posterior wall bulges diffusely, so the sac usually has a **wide neck** [1].
- A **narrow neck** at the deep ring makes indirect hernias more liable to obstruction and strangulation than direct hernias [1].
- **Strangulation** occurs when the blood supply of the contents is cut off: venous return is obstructed first, the contents swell, and arterial supply then fails, leading to gangrene [1].
- A **sliding hernia** forms when a retroperitoneal viscus slides down, so that the viscus itself forms part of the posterior wall of the sac [1].

## Clinical features
- Typical presentation is a groin swelling that appears on standing or straining and disappears on lying down, often with a dragging discomfort [1].
- A reducible hernia has an **expansile cough impulse** [1].
- One **cannot get above** an inguinoscrotal hernia, which distinguishes it from a purely scrotal swelling such as a hydrocele [1].
- **Indirect** hernia: oblique, pear-shaped swelling that commonly descends into the scrotum and reduces upwards, laterally and backwards [1].
- **Direct** hernia: globular swelling near the pubic tubercle that reduces straight backwards, rarely descends into the scrotum, and is often bilateral in elderly men [1].
- **Deep ring occlusion test**: after reduction, pressure over the deep ring (1.25 cm above the midinguinal point) while the patient coughs controls an indirect hernia, whereas a direct hernia still appears medial to the finger [1].
- **Zieman's (three-finger) test**: with the index finger over the deep ring, middle finger over the superficial ring and ring finger over the saphenous opening, an impulse on the index finger suggests indirect, on the middle finger direct, and on the ring finger femoral hernia [1].
- In the **invagination test** the little finger invaginates the scrotal skin into the superficial ring; an impulse on the tip suggests indirect and on the pulp suggests direct hernia [1].
- Contents are commonly **omentum (omentocele)**, which feels doughy, or **small bowel (enterocele)**, which may gurgle on reduction [1].
- **Strangulated** hernia: tense, tender, irreducible swelling with **no cough impulse**, often with features of intestinal obstruction and systemic toxicity [1].

### Special (named) hernias
- **Richter's hernia**: only part of the circumference of the bowel wall (antimesenteric border) is trapped, so it can strangulate without causing complete intestinal obstruction; it is more common with femoral than inguinal hernia [1].
- **Littre's hernia**: a hernia sac containing a **Meckel's diverticulum** [1].
- **Amyand's hernia**: the **appendix** within an **inguinal** hernia sac [1].
- **De Garengeot hernia**: the appendix within a **femoral** hernia sac [1].
- **Maydl's hernia (hernia-en-W)**: two loops in the sac, with the strangulated loop being the intervening loop lying inside the abdomen [1].
- **Sliding hernia (hernia en glissade)**: more common on the **left** (sigmoid colon) and in elderly men; the viscus must not be opened in mistake for the sac [1].

### Nyhus classification

| IIIc | Femoral hernia |

## Investigations
- Inguinal hernia is a **clinical diagnosis**; imaging is not needed when a hernia is clinically evident [1].
- **Ultrasonography** is the first imaging test for an occult hernia or an uncertain groin swelling [1].
- **MRI** is more sensitive than ultrasound for an occult hernia when ultrasound is inconclusive [1].
- **CT** is used for complicated or obscure presentations, such as suspected obstruction or strangulation in an obese patient [1].
- **Herniography** is a historical investigation that has largely been replaced by cross-sectional imaging [1].

## Management
### General principles
- Symptomatic inguinal hernia in a fit adult is treated by elective surgical repair [1].
- **Watchful waiting** is acceptable for men with minimally symptomatic or asymptomatic hernias, although many go on to need surgery because symptoms develop [1].
- A **truss** is reserved for patients unfit or unwilling to have surgery and is not a definitive treatment [1].
- A **strangulated** hernia needs **emergency surgery**, with resection of non-viable bowel [1].
- Forceful manual reduction (**taxis**) is avoided when strangulation is suspected, because it can push gangrenous bowel back or produce **reduction en masse** (sac and contents reduced together with the constriction persisting) [1].

### Children
- Paediatric inguinal hernia is repaired by **herniotomy** alone, because the defect is a patent processus vaginalis with a normal posterior wall [1].
- Paediatric inguinal hernia should be repaired soon after diagnosis because of the high risk of incarceration in infancy [1].
- In girls, an **ovary** in the hernial sac is an indication for early repair because of the risk of torsion [1].
- **Bilateral inguinal hernia in a phenotypic girl** should raise suspicion of **androgen insensitivity syndrome** (testicular feminisation), in which testes may be found in the sacs [1].

### Open repairs in adults
- **Lichtenstein repair**: a **polypropylene mesh** is placed on the posterior wall, sutured inferiorly to the inguinal ligament and superiorly to the internal oblique/conjoint tendon, overlapping the pubic tubercle medially, with a slit laterally for the cord [1].
- Lichtenstein repair is **tension-free**, can be done under local anaesthesia, and has a low recurrence rate [1].
- **Bassini repair** sutures the conjoint tendon to the inguinal ligament (a tissue repair under tension) [1].
- **Shouldice repair** is a multilayer (four-layer) double-breasting repair of the transversalis fascia and is the tissue repair with the lowest recurrence rate [1].
- **McVay (Cooper's ligament) repair** sutures the conjoint tendon to Cooper's (pectineal) ligament and also closes the femoral canal [1].
- **Desarda repair** uses an undetached strip of external oblique aponeurosis to reinforce the posterior wall without mesh [1].
- Tissue (non-mesh) repairs such as Shouldice are used when mesh is not available or not wanted [1].

### Laparoscopic repairs
- **TEP (totally extraperitoneal)** repair places the mesh in the preperitoneal space without entering the peritoneal cavity [1].
- **TAPP (transabdominal preperitoneal)** repair enters the peritoneal cavity, raises a peritoneal flap, places the mesh preperitoneally and closes the flap [1].
- Laparoscopic repair offers less postoperative pain and faster return to activity than open repair but requires general anaesthesia and a longer learning curve [1].
- Laparoscopic repair is preferred for **bilateral** hernias, for **recurrence after open repair**, and is a good option for primary unilateral hernia in experienced hands [1].
- After a failed laparoscopic repair, the recurrence is usually repaired by the **open (anterior)** route, operating through unscarred tissue [1].
- The **triangle of doom** is bounded by the **vas deferens** medially and the **gonadal (testicular) vessels** laterally, and contains the **external iliac vessels**; tacks must not be placed there [1].
- The **triangle of pain** lies lateral to the gonadal vessels and below the iliopubic tract and contains the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, the femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve and the femoral nerve; tacks must not be placed there [1].
- The **corona mortis** is a vascular connection between the obturator and inferior epigastric (or external iliac) vessels crossing Cooper's ligament, which can bleed heavily if injured [1].

## Complications
### Of the hernia
- Irreducibility, obstruction, strangulation and incarceration are the main complications of a hernia [1].
- **Incarceration** classically describes a colon-containing hernia whose lumen is blocked by faeces [1].
- Indirect inguinal hernia carries a higher risk of strangulation than direct inguinal hernia [1].
- Among groin hernias, **femoral hernia** has the highest risk of strangulation [1].

### Of the repair
- **Chronic postoperative inguinal pain** is the most common significant long-term complication of inguinal hernia repair [1].
- The **ilioinguinal nerve** is the nerve most commonly injured in open inguinal hernia repair, causing numbness over the root of the penis, anterior scrotum and upper medial thigh [1].
- In laparoscopic repair the **lateral femoral cutaneous nerve** is the nerve most at risk, causing meralgia paraesthetica [1].
- **Seroma** is a common early complication, particularly after laparoscopic repair, and most resolve without aspiration [1].
- Other complications include haematoma, wound infection, urinary retention, recurrence, mesh infection, and **ischaemic orchitis leading to testicular atrophy** from damage to the cord vessels [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- Deep ring surface marking: PYQs and Indian texts usually expect "1.25 cm above the **midinguinal point**", while some texts give "above the **midpoint of the inguinal ligament**"; choose the option the stem's wording matches, and prefer midinguinal point when both are offered [2].
- Hesselbach's triangle: the original description used **Cooper's (pectineal) ligament** as the base, but exam keys expect the **inguinal ligament** as the inferior boundary [2].
- Femoral hernia is relatively more common in women than in men, but **indirect inguinal hernia is still the most common hernia in women**; options that offer "femoral" for "most common hernia in females" are a trap [2].
- Direct hernia is the classic answer for "hernia that rarely strangulates", and indirect hernia for "inguinal hernia more likely to strangulate"; femoral hernia beats both for "groin hernia most likely to strangulate" [2].
- PYQs have expected Bassini as the classic repair in older questions; current practice is Lichtenstein mesh repair (open) or laparoscopic TEP/TAPP, with Shouldice as the best tissue repair [1].
- Littre's (Meckel's) vs Amyand's (appendix, inguinal) vs de Garengeot's (appendix, femoral) vs Richter's (part of wall) vs Maydl's (W-loop) are a common matching question [2].
