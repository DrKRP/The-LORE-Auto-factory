# Gallstones and acute cholecystitis

> Gallstones (cholelithiasis) are common, and most stay **asymptomatic** for life [1]. Acute cholecystitis is acute inflammation of the gallbladder, in most cases caused by a stone **impacted in the cystic duct or Hartmann's pouch** [1]. The exam tests stone types, risk factors, the imaging of choice, the **Tokyo Guidelines (TG18)** criteria and grading, the timing of cholecystectomy, and the eponymous complications (Mirizzi, gallstone ileus, Courvoisier) [2].

## High-yield
- **Ultrasonography** is the initial investigation of choice for gallstones and for suspected acute cholecystitis [1].
- **HIDA scan (cholescintigraphy)** is the most sensitive and specific test for acute cholecystitis: **non-visualisation of the gallbladder** with tracer reaching the duodenum confirms cystic duct obstruction [1].
- Only about **10–15% of gallstones are radio-opaque** on plain X-ray, unlike most renal stones [1].
- **Murphy's sign** is catching of breath (inspiratory arrest) on deep palpation of the right upper quadrant during inspiration [1].
- **Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy** (during the index admission, ideally within 72 hours of symptom onset) is the current treatment of choice for acute calculous cholecystitis in fit patients [1].
- **Calot's triangle** is bounded by the **cystic duct**, the **common hepatic duct** and the **inferior surface of the liver**; it contains the **cystic artery** [1].
- **Gallstone ileus** most commonly impacts in the **terminal ileum**, and **Rigler's triad** is pneumobilia, small-bowel obstruction and an ectopic (often calcified) gallstone [1].
- **Courvoisier's law**: in a jaundiced patient, a palpable, non-tender gallbladder is unlikely to be due to stones and suggests malignant distal obstruction (e.g. carcinoma head of pancreas) [1].

## Definition
- **Cholelithiasis** is the presence of stones in the gallbladder; **choledocholithiasis** is the presence of stones in the common bile duct [1].
- **Biliary colic** is pain caused by a stone transiently obstructing the gallbladder outlet, without inflammation of the gallbladder wall [1].
- **Acute cholecystitis** is acute inflammation of the gallbladder; about **90–95%** of cases are **calculous** [1].
- **Acalculous cholecystitis** is acute inflammation of the gallbladder in the absence of stones and makes up about **5–10%** of cases [1].

## Causes / Risk factors
### Stone types
- **Cholesterol and mixed stones** together form the large majority (about 80%) of gallstones in Western and Indian practice [1].
- PYQ keys usually give **mixed stones** as the most common type of gallstone [2].
- **Pure cholesterol stones** are typically large, often **solitary**, pale yellow and radiolucent [1].
- **Black pigment stones** are small, multiple and hard, form in the gallbladder, and are associated with **chronic haemolysis** (hereditary spherocytosis, sickle cell disease) and **cirrhosis** [1].
- **Brown pigment stones** are soft and earthy, form in the **bile ducts**, and are associated with **biliary infection and stasis** and with parasites such as *Clonorchis* and *Ascaris* [1].
- Bacterial **β-glucuronidase** deconjugates bilirubin in infected bile, which favours brown pigment stone formation [1].
- Pigment stones contain more calcium and are more often radio-opaque than cholesterol stones [1].

### Risk factors
- The classic mnemonic for cholesterol gallstones is **"female, fat, fertile, forty"** (often with "fair") [1].
- **Female sex, pregnancy, oestrogens and oral contraceptives** increase risk: oestrogen increases biliary cholesterol secretion and progesterone reduces gallbladder motility [1].
- **Obesity** and **rapid weight loss** (including after bariatric surgery) increase risk of cholesterol stones [1].
- **Terminal ileal resection or Crohn's disease of the ileum** interrupts the enterohepatic circulation of bile salts and predisposes to cholesterol stones [1].
- **Total parenteral nutrition**, **octreotide** and **fasting** cause gallbladder stasis and sludge [1].
- **Ceftriaxone** can precipitate in bile and cause biliary sludge (pseudolithiasis) [1].
- **Fibrates (clofibrate)** increase biliary cholesterol secretion and the risk of gallstones [1].
- **Saint's triad** is gallstones, hiatus hernia and colonic diverticulosis [1].

## Pathophysiology
- Cholesterol is held in solution in bile by **bile salts** and **phospholipid (lecithin)**; the **Admirand–Small triangle** plots these three components to show the zone of cholesterol solubility [1].
- Cholesterol stone formation needs **supersaturation of bile with cholesterol**, **nucleation** (crystal formation) and **gallbladder hypomotility (stasis)** [1].
- **Biliary sludge** is a precursor that can resolve, persist or progress to stones [1].
- In acute calculous cholecystitis, a stone obstructs the cystic duct or Hartmann's pouch, and the initial inflammation is **chemical** (e.g. from lysolecithin), with bacterial infection following secondarily [1].
- The organisms most often isolated from infected bile are **gram-negative enteric bacteria**, most commonly ***E. coli***, followed by *Klebsiella*, with *Enterococcus* and anaerobes also found [1].
- Acalculous cholecystitis arises from gallbladder **stasis and ischaemia** in critically ill patients: after major surgery, trauma, **burns**, sepsis, prolonged fasting or TPN, and in the ICU [1].
- Gangrene and perforation are more frequent in acalculous than in calculous cholecystitis, and its mortality is higher [1].

## Clinical features
- Most gallstones (about 80%) are **asymptomatic**, and the risk of developing symptoms is roughly **1–2% per year** [1].
- **Biliary colic** is a misnomer: the pain is usually **constant**, not colicky, felt in the epigastrium or right upper quadrant, often radiating to the **right scapula or shoulder**, frequently after a **fatty meal**, and settles within a few hours (typically under 6 hours) [1].
- Pain persisting beyond about 6 hours, with fever and local tenderness, suggests progression to **acute cholecystitis** [1].
- Acute cholecystitis presents with persistent RUQ pain, **fever**, vomiting, RUQ tenderness with guarding, and a **positive Murphy's sign** [1].
- Pain referred to the **right shoulder tip** arises from diaphragmatic irritation via the **phrenic nerve (C3–C5)** [1].
- **Boas' sign** is an area of hyperaesthesia below the right scapula in acute cholecystitis [1].
- A **tender palpable mass** in the RUQ may be the inflamed gallbladder wrapped in omentum, or an empyema [1].
- Mild jaundice can occur in acute cholecystitis, but **significant jaundice** suggests a **CBD stone**, cholangitis or **Mirizzi syndrome** [1].
- Murphy's sign may be **absent in the elderly** and in diabetics, who can present with few signs despite gangrene [1].

## Investigations
- Blood tests show **leucocytosis** and raised **CRP**; mildly raised bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase or amylase can occur [1].
- **Ultrasound** shows gallstones as **echogenic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing** that move with posture [1].
- Ultrasound features of acute cholecystitis include **gallbladder wall thickening**, **pericholecystic fluid**, a distended gallbladder, an impacted stone in the neck and a **sonographic Murphy's sign** [1].
- Ultrasound detects gallbladder stones with a sensitivity above about **95%** [1].
- **HIDA (Tc-99m iminodiacetic acid) scan**: non-filling of the gallbladder while the tracer enters the duodenum indicates **cystic duct obstruction** and supports acute cholecystitis [1].
- **CT** is used for complications (perforation, abscess, **emphysematous cholecystitis**) and to exclude other diagnoses, but it misses many gallstones [1].
- **MRCP** is the non-invasive investigation of choice to detect **CBD stones** [1].
- **ERCP** is both diagnostic and therapeutic for CBD stones (sphincterotomy and stone extraction) [1].
- **Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)** is highly accurate for small CBD stones [1].
- A plain abdominal X-ray may show radio-opaque stones, a **porcelain gallbladder**, gas in the gallbladder wall (emphysematous cholecystitis) or **pneumobilia** [1].

### Tokyo Guidelines 2018 (TG18): diagnosis of acute cholecystitis
- **A. Local signs of inflammation**: Murphy's sign, or RUQ mass, pain or tenderness [1].
- **B. Systemic signs of inflammation**: fever, raised CRP, or raised white cell count [1].
- **C. Imaging findings** characteristic of acute cholecystitis [1].
- **Suspected diagnosis** = one item in A **plus** one item in B; **definite diagnosis** = A + B + C [1].

### TG18: severity grading
- **Grade III (severe)**: acute cholecystitis with **organ dysfunction** in any one system [1]:
  - Cardiovascular: hypotension needing **dopamine ≥5 µg/kg/min** or any dose of **noradrenaline** [1].
  - Neurological: decreased level of consciousness [1].
  - Respiratory: **PaO2/FiO2 ratio <300** [1].
  - Renal: oliguria or **serum creatinine >2.0 mg/dL** [1].
  - Hepatic: **PT-INR >1.5** [1].
  - Haematological: **platelet count <100,000/mm³** [1].
- **Grade II (moderate)**: any one of **WBC >18,000/mm³**, a **palpable tender RUQ mass**, **duration of complaints >72 hours**, or **marked local inflammation** (gangrenous or emphysematous cholecystitis, pericholecystic or hepatic abscess, biliary peritonitis) [1].
- **Grade I (mild)**: acute cholecystitis that meets neither the Grade II nor the Grade III criteria, in an otherwise healthy patient [1].

## Management
### Asymptomatic gallstones
- Asymptomatic gallstones generally need **no treatment** (expectant management) [1].
- Prophylactic cholecystectomy is considered in selected asymptomatic patients at high risk of complications or cancer, e.g. **porcelain gallbladder**, large stones (**>3 cm**), associated **gallbladder polyp >1 cm**, and **sickle cell disease** [1].
- PYQ keys have also listed **diabetes mellitus** as an indication for prophylactic cholecystectomy in asymptomatic stones [2] ⚠ Unverified

### Symptomatic gallstones
- **Laparoscopic cholecystectomy** is the treatment of choice for symptomatic gallstones [1].
- **Oral dissolution with ursodeoxycholic acid** works only for small, radiolucent **cholesterol** stones in a **functioning** gallbladder, and stones often recur after stopping treatment [1].

### Acute cholecystitis
- Initial management is **nil by mouth**, **IV fluids**, **analgesia** and **IV antibiotics** [1].
- **Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy**, during the same admission and ideally within 72 hours of symptom onset, is preferred over delayed (interval) surgery: it shortens total hospital stay without increasing complications or conversion [1].
- The traditional alternative is **conservative management followed by interval cholecystectomy after about 6–8 weeks** [1].
- In TG18, **Grade I** is treated by early laparoscopic cholecystectomy; **Grade II** by early cholecystectomy in an experienced centre if the patient is fit, otherwise initial medical treatment or gallbladder drainage; **Grade III** by organ support and, in most, **gallbladder drainage** first, with early cholecystectomy only in selected fit patients at advanced centres [1].
- **Percutaneous cholecystostomy** (gallbladder drainage) is used for patients too ill or high-risk for surgery, including many with **acalculous cholecystitis** [1].
- In a difficult gallbladder where Calot's triangle cannot be safely dissected, **subtotal cholecystectomy** or conversion to open surgery is a safe bail-out [1].
- **Emphysematous cholecystitis** needs urgent cholecystectomy (or drainage) and antibiotics because of the high risk of gangrene and perforation [1].

### Safe cholecystectomy
- The **critical view of safety (Strasberg)** has three requirements: the **hepatocystic triangle cleared** of fat and fibrous tissue, the **lower third of the gallbladder separated from the liver bed (cystic plate)**, and **two and only two structures** (cystic duct and cystic artery) seen entering the gallbladder [1].
- The commonest cause of major bile duct injury is **misidentification** of the common bile duct as the cystic duct [1].
- Bile duct injuries are classified by **Strasberg** (laparoscopic era) and **Bismuth** (level of benign stricture) [1].
- The cystic artery is usually a branch of the **right hepatic artery** [1].

## Complications
### Of gallstones
- **Acute cholecystitis**, **chronic cholecystitis**, **choledocholithiasis**, **acute cholangitis**, **acute (gallstone) pancreatitis**, **gallstone ileus** and **carcinoma of the gallbladder** [1].
- Gallstones are the most important risk factor for **gallbladder carcinoma** [1].
- **Gallstones are the commonest cause of acute pancreatitis**, followed by alcohol [1].
- **Mucocele (hydrops)** of the gallbladder: a stone obstructs the cystic duct, bile pigment is absorbed, and the distended gallbladder fills with clear mucus ("**white bile**") [1].
- **Mirizzi syndrome**: a stone impacted in Hartmann's pouch or the cystic duct compresses the **common hepatic duct**, causing obstructive jaundice, and may erode into it to form a **cholecystocholedochal fistula**; it is classified by **Csendes** [1].
- **Acute cholangitis** presents with **Charcot's triad** (fever with rigors, jaundice, RUQ pain); adding **hypotension and confusion** gives **Reynolds' pentad** [1].
- **Porcelain gallbladder** is calcification of the gallbladder wall [1].
- **Strawberry gallbladder** is **cholesterolosis** (lipid-laden macrophages in the mucosa), not a stone disease [1].

### Of acute cholecystitis
- **Empyema** of the gallbladder (pus-filled gallbladder) [1].
- **Gangrene and perforation**, which may be localised (pericholecystic abscess), free (biliary peritonitis) or into an adjacent viscus (**cholecystoenteric fistula**) [1].
- **Emphysematous cholecystitis** is infection with gas-forming organisms, most often ***Clostridium perfringens***, classically in **elderly diabetic men**, with gas in the gallbladder wall or lumen [1].
- The commonest cholecystoenteric fistula is **cholecystoduodenal** [1].
- **Gallstone ileus** is mechanical small-bowel obstruction from a large gallstone that has passed through a cholecystoenteric fistula; it most often lodges in the **terminal ileum** [1].
- Gallstone ileus is commonest in **elderly women** [1].
- **Bouveret's syndrome** is **gastric outlet obstruction** from a gallstone impacted in the duodenum or pylorus [1].
- Gallstone ileus is treated by **enterolithotomy** (removing the stone through a proximal enterotomy); the fistula is usually left for later or not repaired in frail patients [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- PYQs have expected **mixed stones** as the most common type of gallstone; standard texts group cholesterol and mixed stones together as the majority, and some Western sources call "cholesterol stones" the commonest [2][1].
- PYQs have expected **conservative treatment followed by interval cholecystectomy at 6–8 weeks** for acute cholecystitis; current practice (TG18 and standard texts) is **early laparoscopic cholecystectomy** within the index admission [2][1].
- PYQs have asked "**investigation of choice** for acute cholecystitis" with **HIDA scan** as the key (most accurate), while **ultrasound** is the first-line investigation; read the question for "first / initial" versus "most sensitive / most specific / gold standard" [2][1].
- PYQs have expected **diabetes mellitus** as an indication for prophylactic cholecystectomy in asymptomatic gallstones; current practice does not routinely advise it [2] ⚠ Unverified
- **Biliary colic is not colicky**: the pain is steady, and options calling it "intermittent, waves of pain" are a trap [2][1].
- **Courvoisier's law** is about a palpable gallbladder **with jaundice**; a palpable tender gallbladder **without** jaundice in acute cholecystitis (mucocele, empyema) does not contradict it [2][1].
- **Calot's triangle** in modern texts has the **inferior surface of the liver** as its upper border (the hepatocystic triangle); Calot's original description used the **cystic artery** as the upper border [2][1].
