# Varicose veins

> Dilated, tortuous superficial leg veins caused by valve failure and venous reflux, most often of the great saphenous vein at the saphenofemoral junction. A perennial NEET PG surgery topic: venous anatomy and perforators, the bedside tests, duplex scanning, CEAP, and the shift from Trendelenburg-and-stripping to endovenous ablation.

## High-yield
- Varicose veins are dilated, tortuous, elongated superficial veins; in CEAP they are veins **≥3 mm** in diameter measured standing [1].
- The **great saphenous vein (GSV)** system is the most commonly affected, and **saphenofemoral junction (SFJ) incompetence** is the most common site of reflux [1].
- **Duplex ultrasound (colour Doppler)** is the investigation of choice for varicose veins [1].
- **Brodie–Trendelenburg test** detects SFJ incompetence; **Perthes test** checks deep vein patency [1].
- **Endovenous thermal ablation** (radiofrequency or laser) is the current first-line treatment for truncal saphenous reflux; ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy is next, and open surgery is used if both are unsuitable [1].
- **Trendelenburg operation** = flush ligation of the GSV at the SFJ with ligation of all its tributaries [1].
- The GSV is stripped only to just below the knee to avoid **saphenous nerve** injury [1].
- Short saphenous vein surgery risks the **sural nerve** [1].
- **Venous ulcers** occur in the **gaiter area**, most commonly just above the **medial malleolus** [1].
- Compression for a venous ulcer requires an **ABPI ≥0.8** to exclude significant arterial disease [1].

## Definition
- Varicose veins are dilated, tortuous and elongated superficial veins with incompetent valves [1].
- In the CEAP classification, varicose veins (C2) are subcutaneous veins **≥3 mm** in diameter in the upright position [1].
- Telangiectasias (<1 mm) and reticular veins (1–3 mm) form CEAP class C1 [1].

### Applied anatomy
- **Great (long) saphenous vein:** begins at the medial end of the dorsal venous arch, runs **anterior to the medial malleolus**, ascends on the medial side of leg and thigh, and joins the **femoral vein** at the SFJ through the saphenous opening (fossa ovalis) inferolateral to the **pubic tubercle** [1].
- The GSV is the **longest vein** in the body [1].
- The **saphenous nerve** accompanies the GSV in the leg below the knee [1].
- Tributaries at the SFJ include the **superficial epigastric, superficial circumflex iliac and superficial external pudendal** veins, along with the deep external pudendal vein [1].
- **Short (small) saphenous vein:** begins at the lateral end of the dorsal venous arch, runs **behind the lateral malleolus**, and usually joins the **popliteal vein** at the saphenopopliteal junction (SPJ) in the popliteal fossa [1].
- The **sural nerve** accompanies the short saphenous vein [1].
- The level of the SPJ is variable, so it is marked by duplex before surgery [1].
- **Perforating veins** connect superficial to deep veins, and their valves normally allow flow only from superficial to deep [1].

| Perforator (eponym) | Site | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| **Cockett** | Medial lower leg/ankle; connect the posterior arch vein to the posterior tibial veins | [1] |
| **Boyd** | Medial leg just below the knee | [1] |
| **Dodd** | Medial lower thigh (adductor/Hunter's canal region) | [1] |
| **Hunter** | Medial mid-thigh | [1] |

- Current anatomical nomenclature replaces these eponyms with descriptive names (e.g. posterior tibial perforators for Cockett) [1].

## Causes / Risk factors
- **Primary varicose veins** (most common) arise from intrinsic weakness of the vein wall and valves without a deep venous cause [1].
- **Secondary varicose veins** follow deep vein thrombosis (post-thrombotic), pelvic masses or pregnancy obstructing venous outflow, arteriovenous fistula, or congenital malformations such as **Klippel–Trenaunay syndrome** [1].
- Risk factors include **female sex, pregnancy, family history, increasing age, prolonged standing, and obesity** [1].
- In pregnancy, **progesterone**-mediated venous dilatation and pressure of the gravid uterus on pelvic veins both contribute [1].
- Congenital absence of valves is a rare cause [1].

## Pathophysiology
- Valve incompetence allows **reflux** (retrograde flow) of blood from deep to superficial veins or down the superficial trunks [1].
- Normally the **calf muscle pump** lowers venous pressure in the leg during walking [1].
- With reflux or deep obstruction, pressure fails to fall on walking, causing **ambulatory venous hypertension** [1].
- Sustained venous hypertension causes capillary leak of fluid, red cells and fibrinogen, with leukocyte trapping and inflammation, leading to oedema, skin changes and ulceration [1].
- **Haemosiderin** from extravasated red cells causes the brown skin pigmentation [1].

## Clinical features
- Symptoms include **aching, heaviness, itching, cramps and ankle swelling**, worse after prolonged standing and at the end of the day, and relieved by **leg elevation** [1].
- Many patients present for cosmetic reasons with no symptoms [1].
- Skin changes of chronic venous insufficiency include **pigmentation, venous eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche** and **corona phlebectatica** [1].
- **Lipodermatosclerosis** is induration and fibrosis of skin and subcutaneous fat of the lower leg, giving an **"inverted champagne bottle"** leg shape [1].
- **Atrophie blanche** is white, scarred skin with dilated capillary dots, and marks a high risk of ulceration [1].
- **Corona phlebectatica** is a fan of small intradermal veins around the ankle and foot [1].
- **Saphena varix** is a dilatation of the GSV at the SFJ that disappears on lying down, has a **cough impulse** and a **fluid thrill on tapping**, and is a differential for **femoral hernia** [1].

### Bedside tests

| Test | What it shows | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| **Brodie–Trendelenburg test** | Leg elevated, veins emptied, SFJ compressed, patient stands. Rapid filling from above on release of pressure = **SFJ incompetence**; filling with pressure still on = **perforator** incompetence | [1] |
| **Tourniquet test (multiple tourniquet)** | Tourniquets at different levels localise the level of the incompetent perforator | [1] |
| **Perthes test** | Tourniquet applied, patient walks/exercises; veins empty if **deep veins are patent** and perforators competent; increased pain and distension suggest **deep vein obstruction** | [1] |
| **Schwartz (tap) test** | Tap at a lower varicosity felt at the SFJ indicates a continuous column of blood with incompetent valves | [1] |
| **Cough impulse (Morrissey's)** | Impulse at the SFJ on coughing indicates SFJ incompetence | [1] |
| **Fegan's test** | Palpable fascial defects mark the sites of incompetent perforators | [1] |
| **Pratt's test** | Uses bandages to show the sites of incompetent perforators | [1] |

- A positive Perthes test (deep vein obstruction) is a **contraindication** to superficial vein ligation or stripping, because superficial veins are then acting as collaterals [1].

## Investigations
- **Duplex ultrasound** is the **investigation of choice**, showing the anatomy of reflux, junctional incompetence, perforators and deep vein patency [1].
- Reflux on duplex is usually defined as retrograde flow lasting **>0.5 seconds** in superficial veins and **>1 second** in the femoral and popliteal veins [1].
- Hand-held Doppler can detect junctional reflux at the bedside but has been superseded by duplex [1].
- **Venography** is not routine and is reserved for complex deep venous disease or planned deep venous intervention [1].
- Photoplethysmography and air plethysmography measure venous refilling time and global venous function [1].
- **ABPI** should be measured before compression therapy to exclude arterial disease [1].

### CEAP classification (clinical class)

| Class | Meaning | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| C0 | No visible or palpable signs of venous disease | [1] |
| C1 | Telangiectasias or reticular veins | [1] |
| C2 | Varicose veins | [1] |
| C3 | Oedema | [1] |
| C4 | Skin changes: C4a pigmentation or eczema; C4b lipodermatosclerosis or atrophie blanche; C4c corona phlebectatica | [1] |
| C5 | Healed venous ulcer | [1] |
| C6 | Active venous ulcer | [1] |

- CEAP stands for **Clinical, Etiological, Anatomical, Pathophysiological** [1].
- The **2020 CEAP revision** added C4c (corona phlebectatica) and the "r" modifier for recurrent varicose veins (C2r) and recurrent ulcer (C6r) [1].
- The Venous Clinical Severity Score (VCSS) is used to track severity and response to treatment [1].

## Management

### Conservative
- Conservative measures are leg elevation, exercise, weight reduction and avoiding prolonged standing [1].
- **Graduated compression stockings** relieve symptoms and are used when intervention is unsuitable or declined, in pregnancy, and after treatment [1].
- Compression is **contraindicated** in significant peripheral arterial disease (ABPI <0.8) [1].
- NICE advises against offering compression hosiery as treatment for symptomatic varicose veins when interventional treatment is suitable [2].

### Interventional: order of preference for truncal reflux (NICE)
1. **Endothermal ablation** — **radiofrequency ablation (RFA)** or **endovenous laser ablation (EVLA)** — is first-line [2].
2. **Ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy** is offered if endothermal ablation is unsuitable [2].
3. **Open surgery** (ligation and stripping) is offered if neither is suitable [2].

- Endothermal ablation is done under **tumescent local anaesthesia**, which also protects surrounding tissue from heat [1].
- Endovenous heat-induced thrombosis (**EHIT**), thrombus extending from the ablated vein into the deep vein, is a specific complication of thermal ablation [1].
- Non-thermal, non-tumescent options include **cyanoacrylate glue** closure and mechanochemical ablation (MOCA) [1].

### Sclerotherapy
- Sclerotherapy injects a sclerosant that damages the endothelium so the vein fibroses and closes [1].
- Common sclerosants are **sodium tetradecyl sulphate (STS)** and **polidocanol** [1].
- **Foam sclerotherapy** (Tessari technique, mixing sclerosant with air) displaces blood and is more effective than liquid in larger veins [1].
- Liquid sclerotherapy is best for telangiectasias, reticular veins and small residual varicosities [1].
- Complications of sclerotherapy include skin pigmentation, skin necrosis from extravasation, thrombophlebitis, DVT and, with foam, transient visual disturbance or neurological events [1].

### Surgery
- **Trendelenburg operation:** flush ligation of the GSV at the SFJ with ligation of all tributaries, through a groin incision [1].
- The GSV is then **stripped** from the groin to just below the knee [1].
- Stripping below the knee is avoided because of the risk of **saphenous nerve** injury [1].
- **Multiple stab avulsions (ambulatory phlebectomy)** remove residual tributary varicosities [1].
- Short saphenous incompetence is treated by **SPJ ligation** in the popliteal fossa, with risk to the **sural nerve** and the common peroneal nerve [1].
- Incompetent perforators can be ligated by **subfascial endoscopic perforator surgery (SEPS)** or the older open Linton procedure [1].
- Surgery for superficial varicose veins is contraindicated when the deep veins are occluded [1].

### Venous ulcer
- **Multilayer (four-layer) compression bandaging** is the mainstay of venous ulcer treatment [1].
- An **Unna boot** is a zinc-oxide-impregnated compression dressing [1].
- Correcting superficial venous reflux by early endovenous ablation speeds ulcer healing and reduces recurrence (EVRA trial) [1].
- A non-healing ulcer with an atypical edge should be biopsied to exclude **Marjolin's ulcer** (squamous cell carcinoma) [1].

## Complications
### Of varicose veins
- **Haemorrhage** from a ruptured varix, controlled by leg elevation and direct pressure [1].
- **Superficial thrombophlebitis** [1].
- Venous eczema, pigmentation and lipodermatosclerosis [1].
- **Venous ulceration** [1].
- **Marjolin's ulcer** (squamous cell carcinoma) in a long-standing ulcer [1].
- Periostitis, calcification of the veins, and ankle stiffness (equinus) in long-standing disease [1].

### Of treatment
- **Saphenous nerve** injury after below-knee GSV stripping or ablation, causing numbness on the medial leg [1].
- **Sural nerve** injury with short saphenous surgery or ablation [1].
- Haematoma, wound infection, groin lymphocele/lymphatic leak, and DVT [1].
- Injury to the femoral vein or artery at the SFJ is a rare but serious surgical complication [1].
- **Recurrence** after surgery is common, and **neovascularisation** at the ligated SFJ is a leading cause [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- PYQs have expected **Trendelenburg operation with stripping** as the treatment of varicose veins with SFJ incompetence; current practice (NICE) is **endothermal ablation first-line**, then foam sclerotherapy, then surgery [2].
- Brodie–Trendelenburg is a test for **SFJ incompetence**; Perthes is a test for **deep vein patency**; do not swap them [2].
- The "Trendelenburg test" (varicose veins), the "Trendelenburg operation" (SFJ ligation), the Trendelenburg position and the Trendelenburg sign (hip abductor weakness) are different things [2].
- Dodd perforators are in the **thigh** (Hunter's canal region) while Cockett perforators are at the **ankle/lower leg**; Boyd is just **below the knee** [2].
- The GSV runs **in front of the medial malleolus**; the SSV runs **behind the lateral malleolus** [2].
- **Saphena varix** vs **femoral hernia**: both have a cough impulse, but a saphena varix has a fluid thrill on tapping and empties on lying down [2].
- Before stripping or ligation, deep vein patency must be confirmed (Perthes or duplex), because in deep vein occlusion the superficial veins are the collateral outflow [2].
- Sclerosant answers: **sodium tetradecyl sulphate** is the classic answer; polidocanol is an equally accepted modern agent [2].
