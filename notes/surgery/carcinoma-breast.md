# Carcinoma breast

> Malignant epithelial tumour of the breast, now the most common cancer in women in India and worldwide. It is a surgery staple: risk factors, triple assessment, TNM staging, receptor biology, the choice between breast conservation and mastectomy, axillary staging and adjuvant therapy are asked every year.

## High-yield
- Breast cancer is the **most common cancer in women** in India and worldwide [1].
- The **upper outer quadrant** is the most common site, because it holds the most breast tissue (including the axillary tail of Spence) [1].
- **Invasive ductal carcinoma, no special type (NST)** is the most common histological type, forming about 70–80% of cases [1].
- **BRCA1** lies on chromosome **17q** and **BRCA2** on chromosome **13q**; BRCA2 is the one more associated with **male** breast cancer [1].
- **Triple assessment** = clinical examination + imaging (mammography and/or ultrasound) + tissue diagnosis (**core needle biopsy** preferred over FNAC) [1].
- **Axillary lymph node status** is the single most important prognostic factor [1].
- **Peau d'orange** is caused by obstruction of the subdermal (cutaneous) lymphatics with skin oedema, and puts the tumour in **T4b** [1].
- **Inflammatory carcinoma (T4d)** is due to tumour emboli in dermal lymphatics, and is treated with **neoadjuvant chemotherapy → modified radical mastectomy → radiotherapy**, never breast-conserving surgery [1].
- **Ipsilateral supraclavicular** node involvement is **N3c**, not M1, in current AJCC staging [1].
- **Tamoxifen** is the hormonal agent for ER-positive **premenopausal** women; **aromatase inhibitors** (letrozole, anastrozole, exemestane) are preferred in **postmenopausal** women [1].
- **Trastuzumab** targets HER2 and its key toxicity is **cardiotoxicity** (usually reversible, not dose-dependent) [1].
- The **intercostobrachial nerve** is the nerve most often injured/sacrificed in axillary dissection, giving numbness of the medial upper arm; injury to the **long thoracic nerve** gives **winging of the scapula** [1].

## Definition
- Carcinoma breast is a malignant tumour arising from the epithelium of the terminal duct–lobular unit of the breast [1].
- It is **in situ** (DCIS, LCIS) when confined by the basement membrane and **invasive** when it breaches it [1].

## Causes / Risk factors
- **Female sex** and **increasing age** are the strongest risk factors [1].
- Hereditary syndromes: **BRCA1** and **BRCA2** mutations, **Li-Fraumeni** syndrome (**TP53**), **Cowden** syndrome (**PTEN**), **Peutz-Jeghers** syndrome (**STK11**), ataxia-telangiectasia (**ATM**), and hereditary diffuse gastric cancer (**CDH1**, linked to lobular carcinoma) [1].
- BRCA1-associated cancers are typically **triple-negative / basal-like** [1].
- Prolonged unopposed oestrogen exposure raises risk: **early menarche**, **late menopause**, **nulliparity**, **first full-term pregnancy after 30**, and not breastfeeding [1].
- **Combined hormone replacement therapy**, **postmenopausal obesity**, **alcohol** and **ionising radiation to the chest** (especially in youth, e.g. mantle radiotherapy for Hodgkin lymphoma) increase risk [1].
- A personal history of breast cancer, **atypical ductal or lobular hyperplasia** and **LCIS** increase risk; a family history in first-degree relatives increases risk [1].
- Breastfeeding and early first full-term pregnancy are protective [1].
- **Klinefelter syndrome** and BRCA2 mutation are risk factors for **male breast cancer**, which forms about **1%** of all breast cancers [1].
- The **Gail model** estimates a woman's risk of breast cancer from age, reproductive history, biopsies and family history [1].

## Pathophysiology
### Histological types
- **DCIS**: malignant cells confined to ducts; the **comedo** (high-grade) type has central necrosis and **calcifies**, so it is usually detected as **microcalcifications on mammography** [1].
- **LCIS**: often bilateral and multicentric, does not form a lump or calcify, and is a **risk marker** for invasive cancer in either breast [1].
- **Invasive ductal carcinoma (NST)**: the most common type; forms a hard, gritty, stellate mass (**scirrhous** carcinoma) [1].
- **Invasive lobular carcinoma**: cells in single file (**"Indian file"**) due to loss of **E-cadherin**; more often **bilateral and multicentric**, and spreads unusually to **peritoneum, GI tract, ovaries and meninges** [1].
- **Medullary carcinoma**: soft, well-circumscribed, with a prominent **lymphocytic infiltrate**, associated with BRCA1 and triple-negative status [1].
- **Mucinous (colloid) carcinoma**: occurs in older women and has a favourable prognosis [1].
- **Tubular carcinoma**: well-differentiated and has an excellent prognosis [1].
- **Paget's disease of the nipple**: eczematous change of the nipple caused by **Paget cells** (large cells with clear cytoplasm) in the epidermis, almost always with an underlying **DCIS or invasive carcinoma** [1].

### Molecular subtypes
| Subtype | Receptors | Note | Ref |
|---|---|---|---|
| Luminal A | ER+ and/or PR+, HER2−, low Ki-67 | Most common subtype; best prognosis | [1] |
| Luminal B | ER+, HER2+ or high Ki-67 | Worse than luminal A | [1] |
| HER2-enriched | ER−, PR−, HER2+ | Responds to trastuzumab | [1] |
| Basal-like / triple-negative | ER−, PR−, HER2− | BRCA1-linked; aggressive; no hormonal or anti-HER2 target | [1] |

- HER2 is reported **positive** at IHC **3+**; IHC **2+** is equivocal and is resolved by **in situ hybridisation (FISH/ISH)** [1].

### Grading
- Grade uses the **Nottingham (Elston-Ellis) modification of Scarff-Bloom-Richardson**, scoring **tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism and mitotic count** [1].
- **Nottingham Prognostic Index = 0.2 × tumour size (cm) + grade (1–3) + nodal stage (1–3)** [1].

### Spread
- Local spread is along ducts and fascial planes; involvement of **Cooper's (suspensory) ligaments** causes **skin dimpling**, and ductal involvement/fibrosis causes **nipple retraction** [1].
- About **75%** of breast lymph drains to the **axillary nodes**; most of the remainder drains to the **internal mammary** nodes [1].
- Axillary levels are defined by the **pectoralis minor**: **level I** lateral, **level II** behind (including the interpectoral **Rotter's** nodes), **level III** medial (apical/infraclavicular) [1].
- **Bone** is the most common site of distant metastasis, mostly **osteolytic**; lung, liver and brain are other common sites [1].

## Clinical features
- Most commonly a **painless, hard, irregular lump** with ill-defined margins [1].
- Skin signs: **dimpling** (Cooper's ligament), **peau d'orange**, ulceration, **satellite skin nodules**, and **cancer en cuirasse** (diffuse infiltration of the chest wall skin) [1].
- Nipple signs: **recent retraction**, **bloody discharge**, and eczema-like change (Paget's) [1].
- In Paget's disease the change **starts at the nipple and spreads to the areola**, whereas eczema starts on the areola, is often bilateral and itchy, and spares the nipple [1].
- **Fixity to pectoralis major** is tested by examining with the muscle contracted (hands pressed on hips) [1].
- **Inflammatory carcinoma** presents as a red, warm, swollen breast with peau d'orange involving at least **one-third** of the breast, often without a discrete lump, mimicking mastitis [1].

## Investigations
### Imaging
- **Mammography** is the screening tool of choice and the main imaging test in women over about 35–40 years [1].
- Mammographic signs of malignancy are a **spiculated (stellate) mass** and **clustered pleomorphic microcalcifications** [1].
- **Ultrasound** is preferred in women **under 35** and in dense breasts, and distinguishes solid from cystic lesions [1].
- **MRI** is the most sensitive imaging test; it is used for **screening high-risk women (e.g. BRCA carriers)**, assessing **silicone implants**, judging extent of **lobular** carcinoma, and finding the primary in **occult breast cancer** presenting with axillary nodes [1].
- **BI-RADS** categories: 0 incomplete, 1 negative, 2 benign, 3 probably benign (short-interval follow-up), 4 suspicious (biopsy), 5 highly suggestive of malignancy, 6 known biopsy-proven malignancy [1].

### Tissue diagnosis
- **Core needle (trucut) biopsy** is the preferred tissue test because it distinguishes invasive from in situ cancer and gives grade and **ER/PR/HER2** status [1].
- **Stereotactic or ultrasound-guided** biopsy is used for impalpable lesions such as microcalcifications [1].
- FNAC is used mainly for **axillary nodes** and cysts [1].

### Staging work-up
- Routine metastatic work-up is not needed in early asymptomatic disease; it (CT chest/abdomen, bone scan, or PET-CT) is done for locally advanced disease or symptoms [1].
- **CA 15-3** and **CEA** are used for monitoring metastatic disease, not for screening or diagnosis [1].

### Screening in India
- Under India's national NCD programme (NPCDCS / NP-NCD), women aged **30–65** are screened by **clinical breast examination**, rather than population mammography [1].

### TNM staging (AJCC 8th, anatomical)
| Category | Definition | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| Tis | DCIS (LCIS was removed from Tis in the 8th edition) | [1] |
| T1 | ≤ 2 cm | [1] |
| T2 | > 2 cm to ≤ 5 cm | [1] |
| T3 | > 5 cm | [1] |
| T4a | Extension to **chest wall** (ribs, intercostal muscles, serratus anterior; **not pectoralis muscle alone**) | [1] |
| T4b | **Skin**: ulceration, ipsilateral satellite nodules, or **peau d'orange** | [1] |
| T4c | Both T4a and T4b | [1] |
| T4d | **Inflammatory carcinoma** | [1] |
| N1 | Mobile ipsilateral level I/II axillary nodes | [1] |
| N2a | **Fixed or matted** ipsilateral level I/II axillary nodes | [1] |
| N2b | Ipsilateral internal mammary nodes without axillary nodes | [1] |
| N3a | Ipsilateral **infraclavicular** (level III) nodes | [1] |
| N3b | Ipsilateral internal mammary **plus** axillary nodes | [1] |
| N3c | Ipsilateral **supraclavicular** nodes | [1] |
| M1 | Distant metastasis (including contralateral axillary/supraclavicular nodes) | [1] |

- **Locally advanced breast cancer (LABC)** means stage III disease: tumours > 5 cm with nodes, T4 tumours, or N2/N3 nodal disease [1].

## Management
### Principles
- Early breast cancer is treated with **surgery to the breast and axilla**, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy and systemic therapy as indicated by stage and receptor status [1].
- **Breast-conserving surgery (BCS)** (wide local excision with clear margins) plus **whole-breast radiotherapy** gives survival **equivalent** to mastectomy in suitable early cancers [1].
- For invasive cancer treated with BCS, an adequate margin is **"no ink on tumour"** [1].

### Contraindications to breast-conserving surgery
- **Multicentric disease** (tumours in separate quadrants) or **diffuse malignant-appearing microcalcifications** [1].
- **Previous radiotherapy** to the breast or chest wall [1].
- **Pregnancy** when radiotherapy would be needed during pregnancy [1].
- **Persistently positive margins** after re-excision [1].
- **Inflammatory breast carcinoma** [1].
- Relative: a **large tumour relative to breast size** (poor cosmesis) and active **connective tissue disease** (scleroderma, SLE) that increases radiation toxicity [1].

### Mastectomy
- **Halsted radical mastectomy** removes the breast, **pectoralis major and minor**, and all three axillary levels; it is now rarely done [1].
- **Modified radical mastectomy (MRM)** removes the breast and axillary nodes while preserving **pectoralis major** [1].
- MRM variants: **Patey** (removes pectoralis minor), **Scanlon** (divides pectoralis minor), **Auchincloss** (preserves pectoralis minor and clears levels I–II) [1].
- Reconstruction options include implants, the **latissimus dorsi flap**, the pedicled **TRAM flap** (based on the **superior epigastric** vessels), and the free **DIEP flap** [1].

### Axilla
- **Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB)** is the standard axillary staging for **clinically node-negative** invasive cancer, using **radioisotope (Tc-99m sulphur colloid)** and/or **blue dye** (isosulfan blue / methylene blue) [1].
- SLNB is **contraindicated** in **inflammatory breast cancer** and in **biopsy-proven node-positive** axilla, where axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) is done [1].
- After the **ACOSOG Z0011** trial, women with **1–2 positive sentinel nodes** treated with BCS and whole-breast radiotherapy can **omit ALND** [1].
- Standard ALND clears **levels I and II** [1].

### Radiotherapy
- Whole-breast radiotherapy is **mandatory after BCS** [1].
- **Post-mastectomy radiotherapy** is indicated for **≥ 4 positive nodes**, **T3/T4** tumours and **positive margins**, and is considered for 1–3 positive nodes [1].

### Systemic therapy
- **Neoadjuvant chemotherapy** is used for **LABC** and **inflammatory** cancer, and to downstage operable tumours to allow BCS [1].
- Standard chemotherapy is **anthracycline- and taxane-based** [1].
- **Tamoxifen** (a SERM) for **5–10 years** is used for hormone-receptor-positive **premenopausal** women [1].
- **Aromatase inhibitors** are preferred for hormone-receptor-positive **postmenopausal** women, because postmenopausal oestrogen comes from peripheral aromatisation [1].
- Tamoxifen side effects: **endometrial carcinoma**, **thromboembolism (DVT/PE)**, hot flushes and **cataract** [1].
- Aromatase inhibitor side effects: **osteoporosis/fractures** and **arthralgia** [1].
- **Trastuzumab** (anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody) is given for **1 year** in HER2-positive disease; its cardiotoxicity is usually **reversible and not dose-related**, unlike the **dose-dependent** cardiomyopathy of **anthracyclines (doxorubicin)** [1].
- Gene-expression assays such as **Oncotype DX (21-gene recurrence score)** guide whether ER-positive, HER2-negative early cancers need chemotherapy [1].

### Special situations
- **DCIS**: treated with **BCS + radiotherapy** or **mastectomy**; routine ALND is not done, and **SLNB is done if mastectomy** is performed [1].
- **Classic LCIS**: managed with **surveillance** and consideration of **chemoprevention**, not mastectomy [1].
- **Paget's disease**: treated according to the underlying cancer, by mastectomy or central BCS (excision of nipple-areola complex) with radiotherapy [1].
- **Inflammatory carcinoma**: **neoadjuvant chemotherapy → MRM → post-mastectomy radiotherapy** (trimodality) [1].
- **Pregnancy-associated breast cancer**: **MRM** is preferred in the first trimester; chemotherapy is safe in the **second and third trimesters**; **radiotherapy, tamoxifen and trastuzumab are avoided** during pregnancy [1].
- **Male breast cancer**: usually treated by **mastectomy with axillary staging**; most are hormone-receptor-positive and receive **tamoxifen** [1].
- **Metastatic disease**: treated primarily with systemic therapy (endocrine first-line if hormone-receptor-positive without visceral crisis), with **bisphosphonates or denosumab** for bone metastases [1].

### Prevention in high-risk women
- **Chemoprevention** uses **tamoxifen**, **raloxifene** (postmenopausal; no increase in endometrial cancer) or aromatase inhibitors [1].
- **Bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy** in BRCA carriers reduces breast cancer risk by about **90%** [1].

## Complications
- Of axillary dissection: **lymphoedema of the arm**, seroma, shoulder stiffness, and nerve injury [1].
- **Intercostobrachial nerve** injury → numbness of the medial upper arm/axilla [1].
- **Long thoracic nerve (of Bell)** injury → paralysis of **serratus anterior** → **winging of the scapula** [1].
- **Thoracodorsal nerve** injury → weakness of **latissimus dorsi** [1].
- **Stewart-Treves syndrome** is **lymphangiosarcoma** arising in a chronically lymphoedematous arm after mastectomy/ALND [1].
- **Seroma** is the most common early complication after mastectomy [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- PYQs have expected **carcinoma cervix** as the most common cancer in Indian women; breast cancer has since overtaken it and is now the most common [2].
- PYQs have expected **FNAC** as the investigation of choice for a breast lump; current practice is **core needle biopsy**, which gives histology and receptor status [2].
- Older classifications and PYQs counted **supraclavicular nodes** as **M1**; current AJCC staging places them in **N3c** [2].
- Older keys gave **radical (Halsted) mastectomy** for operable cancer; current practice is **BCS + radiotherapy** or **MRM** [2].
- Exam trap: **peau d'orange** is **T4b**, not T4d; **T4d** needs the clinical picture of inflammatory carcinoma [2].
- Exam trap: invasion of **pectoralis major alone** is **not T4a** chest-wall involvement [1].
- Exam trap: the cardiotoxicity of **trastuzumab** is reversible and not dose-dependent, while that of **doxorubicin** is dose-dependent and largely irreversible [1].
- Exam trap: **tamoxifen** raises the risk of endometrial cancer, but **raloxifene** does not [1].
- Exam trap: the **most common cause of bloody nipple discharge** is **intraductal papilloma**, not carcinoma [1].
