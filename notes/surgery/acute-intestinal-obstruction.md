# Acute intestinal obstruction

> Failure of intestinal contents to pass onward, either from a physical block (mechanical, "dynamic") or from failed peristalsis (adynamic: paralytic ileus, pseudo-obstruction) [1]. It is one of the commonest surgical emergencies, and the exam centres on the cause by age and site, the X-ray signs, recognising strangulation, and when to operate [1].

## High-yield
- The four cardinal features are **colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, abdominal distension and absolute constipation** (obstipation) [1].
- **Adhesions** (post-operative) are the most common cause of small bowel obstruction in adults [1].
- **Colorectal carcinoma** is the most common cause of large bowel obstruction in adults [1].
- **Intussusception** is the most common cause of intestinal obstruction between 6 months and 2 years of age [1].
- Small bowel is identified on X-ray by **valvulae conniventes** that cross the full width of the bowel; large bowel by **haustra** that do not cross the full width [1].
- **CT abdomen (contrast-enhanced)** is the investigation of choice for small bowel obstruction: it shows the level, the cause and signs of strangulation [1].
- **Strangulation** is suggested by constant (not colicky) pain, tachycardia, fever, localised tenderness or guarding, leucocytosis and a raised lactate [1].
- **Sigmoid volvulus** gives the "coffee bean" / "bent inner tube" sign on plain X-ray [1].
- **Gallstone ileus** shows **Rigler's triad**: pneumobilia, small bowel obstruction and an ectopic (usually right lower quadrant) gallstone [1].
- The **caecum** is the commonest site of perforation in large bowel obstruction with a competent ileocaecal valve, because it has the largest diameter (Laplace's law) [1].

## Definition
- **Mechanical (dynamic) obstruction**: peristalsis works against a physical obstruction [1].
- **Adynamic obstruction**: there is no physical block, but peristalsis is absent (paralytic ileus) or ineffective (pseudo-obstruction) [1].
- **Simple obstruction**: the blood supply of the bowel is intact [1].
- **Strangulated obstruction**: the blood supply of the involved segment is compromised, leading to ischaemia and gangrene [1].
- **Closed-loop obstruction**: a segment of bowel is obstructed at both ends, as in volvulus, a strangulated hernia, or large bowel obstruction with a competent ileocaecal valve [1].
- **Acute, chronic, and acute-on-chronic** obstruction are distinguished by onset; acute obstruction is usually small bowel and chronic obstruction usually large bowel [1].

## Causes / Risk factors
Causes are classified by their relation to the bowel wall [1].

**Intraluminal**
- Faecal impaction, gallstone (gallstone ileus), food bolus, bezoar, and roundworm (*Ascaris*) bolus in children [1].

**Intramural**
- Carcinoma, stricture (Crohn's disease, tuberculosis, ischaemia, diverticular disease), congenital atresia and intussusception [1].
- **Ileocaecal tuberculosis** causing stricture is an important cause of small bowel obstruction in India [1].

**Extramural**
- Adhesions and bands, obstructed or strangulated hernia, volvulus, and external compression by tumour or abscess [1].

**By age and site**

| Setting | Leading cause | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| Neonate | Atresia, malrotation with midgut volvulus, meconium ileus, Hirschsprung's disease | [1] |
| Infant 6 months–2 years | Intussusception | [1] |
| Adult small bowel | Adhesions, then hernia | [1] |
| Adult large bowel | Colorectal carcinoma, then diverticular disease and volvulus | [1] |
| Adult, no previous surgery ("virgin abdomen") | Hernia (check all hernial orifices), then tumour | [1] |

**Specific causes**
- **Hernia**: always examine the hernial orifices in a patient with intestinal obstruction [1].
- **Femoral hernia** carries the highest risk of strangulation among groin hernias because of its narrow, rigid neck [1].
- **Richter's hernia** traps only part of the circumference of the bowel wall, so it can strangulate without producing complete obstruction [1].
- **Littre's hernia** contains a Meckel's diverticulum [1].
- **Maydl's hernia** contains a W-shaped loop, and the strangulated loop lies inside the abdomen [1].
- **Obturator hernia** is typically seen in elderly thin women and may present with pain along the medial thigh (**Howship–Romberg sign**) [1].
- **Volvulus**: the **sigmoid colon** is the commonest site of colonic volvulus, followed by the caecum [1].
- Sigmoid volvulus is favoured by a long, redundant sigmoid on a narrow mesenteric base, chronic constipation and a high-fibre diet, and is common in elderly men and in the "volvulus belt" (Africa, South Asia, South America) [1].
- **Gallstone ileus**: a large gallstone enters the bowel through a cholecystoenteric fistula, most often cholecystoduodenal, and impacts most often in the **terminal ileum** [1].
- **Intussusception**: in children it is usually idiopathic and **ileocolic**, often linked to hypertrophied Peyer's patches after a viral infection [1].
- **Adult intussusception** usually has a pathological lead point, often a tumour [1].
- **Colorectal carcinoma**: tumours of the left colon, and especially the **splenic flexure**, are the most likely to obstruct because the lumen is narrower and the stool more solid [1].

## Pathophysiology
- Proximal to the block the bowel dilates with gas and fluid, while distal bowel empties and collapses [1].
- Most of the gas in the obstructed bowel is **swallowed air** (mainly nitrogen) [1].
- Fluid accumulates in the lumen and the bowel wall because absorption falls and secretion rises, and it is lost from the circulation (third-space loss) [1].
- Vomiting and sequestration together cause **dehydration, hypovolaemia and electrolyte loss** (sodium, potassium, chloride) [1].
- Stasis allows bacterial overgrowth, which makes the contents faeculent and allows bacterial translocation [1].
- Rising intraluminal pressure first obstructs venous return, then arterial inflow, causing ischaemia, gangrene and perforation [1].
- In strangulation the patient develops **metabolic acidosis** with a raised lactate, and toxic products enter the peritoneal cavity and circulation [1].
- **Paralytic ileus** is most commonly seen after abdominal surgery and is worsened by **hypokalaemia**, peritonitis, retroperitoneal haematoma and drugs such as opioids [1].
- After abdominal surgery, small bowel motility returns first (within about 24 hours), the stomach in 24–48 hours and the colon last (48–72 hours) [1].

## Clinical features
**Symptoms**
- **Colicky central abdominal pain** is usually the first symptom of small bowel obstruction [1].
- Pain that becomes **constant and severe** suggests strangulation [1].
- **Vomiting** is early and prominent in high (proximal) small bowel obstruction and late in low small bowel and large bowel obstruction [1].
- Vomitus progresses from gastric content to bile-stained and, in late low obstruction, to **faeculent** vomiting [1].
- **Distension** is minimal in high small bowel obstruction and marked in low small bowel and large bowel obstruction [1].
- **Absolute constipation** (no flatus or faeces) is early in large bowel obstruction and late in small bowel obstruction, as the distal bowel first empties its contents [1].

**Comparison**

| Feature | High small bowel | Low small bowel | Large bowel | Ref |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pain | Colicky, central, early | Colicky, central | Lower abdominal, less severe | [1] |
| Vomiting | Early, profuse | Later, may be faeculent | Late | [1] |
| Distension | Minimal | Moderate, central | Marked, flanks | [1] |
| Constipation | Late | Late | Early | [1] |

**Signs**
- Dehydration (dry tongue, tachycardia, hypotension, oliguria) is common [1].
- Visible peristalsis may be seen, especially in thin patients [1].
- **Bowel sounds** are hyperactive and high-pitched ("tinkling") in mechanical obstruction and absent in paralytic ileus or late peritonitis [1].
- Fever, tachycardia, localised tenderness, guarding and rebound suggest **strangulation or peritonitis** [1].
- A strangulated hernia is **tense, tender, irreducible and has no cough impulse** [1].
- Digital rectal examination may show an empty rectum, a rectal tumour, or faecal impaction [1].

**Intussusception (child)**
- The child has intermittent screaming attacks with drawing up of the legs, vomiting, and passes **"red currant jelly" stool** (blood and mucus) [1].
- A **sausage-shaped mass** may be felt, with an empty right iliac fossa (**Dance's sign**) [1].

## Investigations
**Bloods**
- Full blood count, urea, creatinine, electrolytes, arterial blood gas and **serum lactate** are needed; leucocytosis and a raised lactate suggest strangulation [1].
- Repeated vomiting from a high obstruction can produce **hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis** [1].

**Plain abdominal X-ray**
- A supine abdominal film is used to identify which bowel is distended; an erect film shows **air–fluid levels** [1].
- An erect chest X-ray is taken to look for free gas under the diaphragm (perforation) [1].
- Small bowel loops lie centrally, and the **jejunum** shows valvulae conniventes crossing the full width ("concertina" / "ladder" / "stack of coins" pattern) [1].
- The distended **ileum** is featureless ("characterless") [1].
- Large bowel lies peripherally and shows **haustra** that do not cross the full width [1].
- The **3–6–9 rule** gives the upper normal diameter: small bowel 3 cm, colon 6 cm, caecum 9 cm [2].
- **String of beads sign**: small gas bubbles trapped between valvulae conniventes in fluid-filled small bowel loops [1].
- **Sigmoid volvulus**: a massively dilated loop arising from the pelvis, the "**coffee bean**", "**bent inner tube**" or "**omega**" sign [1].
- **Caecal volvulus**: a dilated, comma- or kidney-shaped caecum displaced towards the left upper quadrant, with dilated small bowel [1].
- **Gallstone ileus**: **Rigler's triad** of pneumobilia, small bowel obstruction and an ectopic gallstone [1].

**CT**
- **Contrast-enhanced CT** is the investigation of choice for suspected small bowel obstruction [1].
- CT shows a **transition point** between dilated and collapsed bowel, the cause, and closed-loop obstruction [1].
- CT signs of strangulation include reduced bowel-wall enhancement, wall thickening, mesenteric oedema, free fluid, pneumatosis intestinalis and portal venous gas [1].
- A "**whirl sign**" of twisted mesentery and vessels indicates volvulus [1].

**Contrast studies**
- An oral **water-soluble contrast** (Gastrografin) study in adhesive small bowel obstruction is both diagnostic and therapeutic [1].
- Contrast reaching the colon on an abdominal X-ray within **24 hours** predicts that the obstruction will resolve without surgery [1].
- Barium must not be given by mouth in suspected large bowel obstruction or perforation [1].
- A water-soluble contrast enema distinguishes mechanical large bowel obstruction from pseudo-obstruction [1].
- In sigmoid volvulus a contrast enema shows a tapering "**bird's beak**" at the twist [1].

**Intussusception**
- **Ultrasound** is the investigation of choice for intussusception in children and shows a "**target**" or "**doughnut**" sign in transverse section and a "**pseudokidney**" sign in longitudinal section [1].
- A contrast enema shows the "**claw sign**" at the apex of the intussusceptum [1].

## Management
**Resuscitation ("drip and suck")**
- **Intravenous fluids** (crystalloid such as Ringer's lactate or 0.9% saline) with potassium replacement as needed [1].
- **Nasogastric tube** decompression to empty the stomach, relieve vomiting and reduce the risk of aspiration [1].
- **Urinary catheter** to monitor urine output, nil by mouth, and analgesia [1].
- Antibiotics are given when strangulation or perforation is suspected and before surgery [1].

**Non-operative management**
- **Adhesive small bowel obstruction** without signs of strangulation or peritonitis is first treated non-operatively [1].
- If adhesive obstruction does not resolve within about **72 hours** of non-operative treatment, surgery is indicated [1].
- **Paralytic ileus** is treated non-operatively by correcting the cause, especially electrolyte (potassium) imbalance, with drip and suck [1].

**Indications for urgent surgery**
- Signs of **strangulation or peritonitis** [1].
- **Obstructed or strangulated hernia** [1].
- **Closed-loop obstruction** [1].
- Failure of non-operative treatment [1].
- Obstruction in a "virgin abdomen" (no previous surgery), where adhesions are unlikely and a surgical cause must be found [1].

**At operation: bowel viability**
- Viable bowel has a normal colour and sheen, visible peristalsis and pulsating mesenteric vessels [1].
- Doubtful bowel is wrapped in warm, moist packs and re-examined after several minutes [1].
- Non-viable bowel is resected; if viability remains doubtful, a planned **second-look laparotomy** at 24–48 hours is done [1].
- Adhesions causing obstruction are divided (**adhesiolysis**), by laparotomy or, in selected patients, laparoscopically [1].

**Specific causes**
- **Sigmoid volvulus without gangrene or peritonitis**: endoscopic detorsion (flexible or rigid sigmoidoscopy) and insertion of a **flatus tube** [1].
- Because sigmoid volvulus recurs often after detorsion, **elective sigmoid colectomy** is advised, usually during the same admission [1].
- **Sigmoid volvulus with gangrene, perforation or failed detorsion**: emergency resection, usually **Hartmann's procedure** [1].
- **Caecal volvulus**: **right hemicolectomy** is preferred; endoscopic detorsion usually fails [1].
- **Gallstone ileus**: **enterolithotomy** (removal of the stone through a proximal longitudinal enterotomy) is the standard emergency operation, with the fistula usually left for later [1].
- **Intussusception in a child without peritonitis**: **non-operative reduction** by air (pneumatic) or saline/contrast (hydrostatic) enema under imaging guidance [1].
- Surgery for paediatric intussusception is needed for peritonitis, perforation, shock, or failed enema reduction [1].
- **Adult intussusception** is treated by resection, because a malignant lead point is common [1].
- **Obstructing right-sided colon cancer**: right (or extended right) hemicolectomy with primary anastomosis [1].
- **Obstructing left-sided colon cancer**: resection with **Hartmann's procedure**, or primary anastomosis in selected stable patients [1].
- A **self-expanding metallic stent** can relieve malignant left-sided obstruction as a bridge to elective surgery or as palliation [1].
- **Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie's syndrome)**: treat the cause and correct electrolytes; if the colon stays dilated, give **intravenous neostigmine** with cardiac monitoring, then colonoscopic decompression if that fails [1].
- Neostigmine can cause **bradycardia**, so atropine should be available [1].

## Complications
- Dehydration, electrolyte imbalance and acute kidney injury [1].
- **Strangulation, gangrene and perforation**, leading to peritonitis and sepsis [1].
- **Aspiration pneumonia** from vomiting [1].
- **Caecal perforation** in closed-loop large bowel obstruction; a caecal diameter above about 12 cm is a warning of impending perforation [1] ⚠ Unverified.
- **Short bowel syndrome** after extensive resection [1].
- Recurrent adhesive obstruction [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- PYQs have expected **obstructed/strangulated hernia** as the most common cause of intestinal obstruction in India and developing countries, while **adhesions** are the most common cause in the West and in current practice wherever abdominal surgery is common [2].
- The old dictum "**never let the sun rise and set on small bowel obstruction**" is still quoted, but current practice gives adhesive obstruction without strangulation a non-operative trial of up to about 72 hours [1][2].
- PYQs have keyed the erect abdominal X-ray as the film showing air–fluid levels; current practice uses the supine film to identify the distended bowel and CT for definitive diagnosis [1][2].
- Do not confuse the X-ray signs: **coffee bean / bent inner tube** = sigmoid volvulus; **bird's beak** = volvulus on contrast enema; **claw sign** = intussusception on enema; **target / doughnut** = intussusception on ultrasound; **string of beads** = small bowel obstruction [2].
- Rigler's **triad** (gallstone ileus) is different from Rigler's **sign** (both sides of the bowel wall visible because of pneumoperitoneum) [2].
- Rigid sigmoidoscopy with a flatus tube is the classic textbook answer for detorsion of sigmoid volvulus; flexible sigmoidoscopy is now commonly used [1][2].
- More than a few air–fluid levels on an erect film are abnormal in adults, but exact cut-offs quoted in exam keys (more than 3, or more than 5) vary between sources [2] ⚠ Unverified.
- Intravenous neostigmine for Ogilvie's syndrome is commonly given as 2 mg intravenously [1] ⚠ Unverified.

## Unverified claims
- Caecal diameter above about 12 cm as a warning of impending perforation — threshold quoted differently across texts (10–12 cm); knowledge mode, not confident of a single figure.
- Number of air–fluid levels considered abnormal (more than 3 vs more than 5) — exam keys vary; knowledge mode.
