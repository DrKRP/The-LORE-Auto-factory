# Burns: assessment and fluid resuscitation

> Burns are assessed by depth, % total body surface area (TBSA), age and inhalation injury. Large burns cause burn shock from capillary leak, which is treated with calculated crystalloid (Ringer lactate) titrated to urine output. The Parkland formula and the rule of nines are the most-tested items. [1]

## High-yield
- **Parkland (Baxter) formula**: 4 mL x body weight (kg) x %TBSA burned of **Ringer lactate** over 24 h; half in the first 8 h counted from the time of burn, half in the next 16 h. [1]
- Maintenance fluid is added in children, and the formula is only a starting estimate to be titrated to urine output. [1]
- **Rule of nines** (adult): head and neck 9, each upper limb 9, anterior trunk 18, posterior trunk 18, each lower limb 18, perineum 1. [1]
- Child (infant) head is about 18% and each lower limb about 14%, so the adult rule of nines overestimates the legs and underestimates the head in children; the **Lund and Browder chart** corrects for age. [1]
- Patient's palm including fingers is about **1% TBSA**, used for small or patchy burns. [1]
- Urine output target: **0.5 mL/kg/h** in adults (30-50 mL/h) and **1 mL/kg/h** in children. [1]
- Simple erythema (superficial/first-degree) is **not counted** in TBSA for resuscitation. [1]
- Inhalation injury and carbon monoxide poisoning are the leading cause of early death at the scene; give **100% oxygen**. [1]
- Circumferential full-thickness burns of the limb or chest need **escharotomy**. [1]

## Definition
- A burn is tissue injury from heat, chemicals, electricity or radiation; thermal burns include scald, flame, contact and flash. [1]
- A **major burn** needing formal IV resuscitation is generally more than 15% TBSA in adults and more than 10% in children (some texts quote 20% for adults). [1]

## Causes / Risk factors
- Scalds are the commonest cause in children; flame burns are commonest in adults overall. [1]
- In India, domestic flame and kerosene-stove or sari-related burns in young women are a classic forensic and surgical problem. [1]
- Risk factors: extremes of age, epilepsy, alcohol or drugs, unsafe cooking, child abuse (look for stocking or glove distribution, sparing of flexures, delay in presentation). [1]

## Pathophysiology
- **Jackson's zones**: zone of **coagulation** (central, irreversible necrosis), zone of **stasis** (potentially salvageable, may convert to deep burn if hypoperfused or infected), zone of **hyperaemia** (outer, recovers). [1]
- Local burn releases histamine, prostaglandins, oxygen free radicals and other mediators that raise capillary permeability; the leak is maximal in the first 8-12 h and continues up to 24 h. [1]
- Large burns (above about 30% TBSA) cause systemic capillary leak even in uninjured tissue, with loss of plasma volume, haemoconcentration (rising haematocrit), hypoalbuminaemia and oedema. [1]
- Burn shock is predominantly **hypovolaemic**, with added myocardial depression from circulating mediators. [1]
- After 24-48 h there is a hypermetabolic, hyperdynamic state with raised catecholamines, cortisol and glucagon, and marked catabolism that persists for months. [1]
- Early hyperkalaemia and acidosis can result from tissue destruction and haemolysis in deep burns. [1]

## Clinical features
- **Depth classification** (traditional degrees and modern terms): [1]
  - Superficial (first degree): epidermis only; red, painful, dry, blanches, no blisters; heals in about 7 days without scar. [1]
  - Superficial partial thickness (superficial second degree): blisters, moist, pink, very painful, blanches; heals in 2-3 weeks. [1]
  - Deep partial thickness (deep second degree): mottled red-white, less painful, sluggish capillary refill, may heal in more than 3 weeks with scarring and often needs grafting. [1]
  - Full thickness (third degree): white, brown or leathery, dry, **painless** (insensate), does not blanch; needs excision and grafting. [1]
  - Fourth degree: involves fascia, muscle or bone. [1]
- The commonest error in assessment is underestimating depth, which declares itself over 48-72 h. [1]
- Features of **inhalation injury**: burns in a closed space, singed nasal hair or eyebrows, facial burns, carbonaceous sputum, hoarseness, stridor, wheeze, soot in the oropharynx. [1]
- **Carbon monoxide poisoning**: headache, confusion, cherry-red skin (late and unreliable); pulse oximetry reads falsely normal because it cannot tell carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin. [1]
- Signs of inadequate resuscitation: tachycardia, low urine output, rising lactate and base deficit, rising haematocrit. [1]

## Investigations
- Primary survey (ABCDE as in ATLS) comes first, with cervical spine protection if trauma is suspected; burn assessment is done in the secondary survey. [1]
- **Carboxyhaemoglobin level** and arterial blood gas (SaO2 on co-oximetry, lactate, base deficit). [1]
- **Fibreoptic bronchoscopy** is the best test to confirm inhalation injury (soot, oedema, erythema below the cords). [1]
- Baseline: CBC, urea and electrolytes, glucose, creatinine, creatine kinase and urine myoglobin in electrical or deep burns, ECG in electrical burns, chest X-ray (often normal early). [1]
- Estimate TBSA with the rule of nines, Lund and Browder chart or palm method; count only partial-thickness and full-thickness areas. [1]

## Management
**Pre-hospital and first aid**
- Stop the burning process, remove clothing and jewellery, and cool the burn with **cool running tap water for 20 minutes**, ideally within 3 hours of injury; do not use ice and avoid hypothermia in large burns. [1]
- Cover with clean cling film or dry sterile sheet; high-flow 100% oxygen if inhalation or CO exposure is suspected. [1]

**Airway**
- **Early intubation** before oedema develops if there is stridor, hoarseness, deep facial or neck burns, extensive oropharyngeal soot or a falling level of consciousness. [1]
- In CO poisoning, 100% oxygen reduces the COHb half-life from about 4-5 h on room air to about 1 h; hyperbaric oxygen reduces it further and is reserved for selected cases. [1]
- Suspect cyanide poisoning (smoke from burning plastics) with unexplained lactic acidosis or shock; treat with **hydroxocobalamin**. [1]

**Fluid resuscitation**
- Start IV fluids (two large-bore cannulas, through unburnt skin if possible) for adults with more than 15% TBSA and children with more than 10%. [1]
- **Parkland formula**: 4 mL x kg x %TBSA of Ringer lactate in 24 h; first half over the first 8 h from the time of injury (not time of arrival), second half over the next 16 h. [1]
- Worked example: 70 kg adult, 40% TBSA gives 4 x 70 x 40 = 11,200 mL in 24 h; 5,600 mL in the first 8 h (700 mL/h) and 5,600 mL in the next 16 h (350 mL/h). [1]
- Fluid already given before arrival is deducted from the first-8-hour volume, and the rate is **titrated hourly** to urine output rather than followed rigidly. [1]
- The Parkland formula uses crystalloid only; **no colloid in the first 24 h** in the classic Parkland protocol, with colloid (albumin) considered after the capillary leak settles (about 8-24 h) or when requirements are very high. [1]
- Current ATLS and ABA-type practice starts lower, at about 2 mL/kg/%TBSA/24 h in adults (3 mL in children), then adjusts to urine output, to limit "fluid creep". [1] ⚠ Unverified
- The ABLS "rule of tens" estimates the initial rate in mL/h as %TBSA x 10 for adults 40-80 kg, adding 100 mL/h for every 10 kg above 80 kg. [1] ⚠ Unverified
- Children need **maintenance fluid** (dextrose-containing) in addition to the calculated resuscitation volume because of small glycogen stores. [1]
- Other formulae: **Brooke** (1.5 mL crystalloid + 0.5 mL colloid /kg/%TBSA plus 2 L dextrose in 5% water), **Evans** (1 mL crystalloid + 1 mL colloid /kg/%TBSA plus 2 L dextrose), **modified Brooke** (2 mL Ringer lactate/kg/%TBSA, no colloid). [1]
- **Monitoring**: urine output hourly with a catheter (0.5 mL/kg/h adult, 1 mL/kg/h child), heart rate, blood pressure, base deficit and lactate; if urine output is low, increase fluid by about 20-25%, and if high, reduce the rate. [1]
- Over-resuscitation causes pulmonary oedema, abdominal compartment syndrome and compartment syndromes in limbs. [1]
- **Electrical burns** and rhabdomyolysis: increase fluids to maintain urine output at about 1-1.5 mL/kg/h (higher target, about 75-100 mL/h in adults), with urine alkalinisation not routinely required if output is adequate. [1]
- Ringer lactate is preferred over normal saline because large volumes of saline cause hyperchloraemic acidosis. [1]

**Wound and supportive care**
- Analgesia with titrated IV opioids; tetanus prophylaxis; nasogastric tube in burns above about 20% TBSA because of ileus. [1]
- **Escharotomy** for circumferential full-thickness limb burns (distal ischaemia, loss of pulses) and chest burns (restricted ventilation); incisions are along the midlateral and midaxial lines through eschar only, and can be done at the bedside without anaesthesia since full-thickness skin is insensate. [1]
- Topical agents: **silver sulfadiazine** (broad spectrum, painless; causes transient leucopenia, avoided in sulfa allergy and on the face), **mafenide acetate** (penetrates eschar, good for ear cartilage; causes metabolic acidosis through carbonic anhydrase inhibition and is painful), **silver nitrate 0.5%** (stains black, causes hyponatraemia and hypochloraemia), **povidone iodine**. [1]
- Early excision and grafting within the first days (typically 24-72 h to first week) reduces sepsis and mortality in deep burns. [1]
- **Early enteral feeding** (within 6-24 h) with high protein and calories reduces gut-barrier failure; the Curreri formula estimates caloric needs. [1]
- Stress ulcer prophylaxis for **Curling's ulcer** (acute gastroduodenal ulcer after major burns). [1]
- Avoid **succinylcholine** from about 24-48 h after a major burn because of hyperkalaemia; non-depolarising blockers are needed at higher doses. [1]
- Chemical burns: copious water irrigation for prolonged periods, remove contaminated clothes; hydrofluoric acid burns are treated with **calcium gluconate** gel or injection. [1]
- Referral criteria to a burn centre include partial-thickness burns above 10% TBSA, burns of face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum or major joints, full-thickness burns, electrical or chemical burns, inhalation injury, and burns at the extremes of age. [1]

## Complications
- Early: hypovolaemic shock, airway obstruction, CO and cyanide poisoning, compartment syndrome, acute kidney injury (myoglobinuria, hypovolaemia), arrhythmias, hypothermia. [1]
- Late: **burn wound sepsis** (Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the classic most common organism in later burn infection; Staphylococcus aureus and streptococci early), pneumonia, Curling's ulcer, hypermetabolism and malnutrition, venous thrombosis. [1]
- Sepsis is the commonest cause of death after the first 48-72 h in patients who survive resuscitation. [1]
- Scars: hypertrophic scars and **keloid**, contractures, and **Marjolin's ulcer** (squamous cell carcinoma arising in a chronic burn scar). [1]

## ⚠ Exam notes
- PYQs have expected the Parkland formula as 4 mL/kg/%TBSA with Ringer lactate, half in the first 8 h; current ATLS and ABA-style practice starts lower (about 2 mL/kg/%TBSA in adults) and titrates to urine output to avoid fluid creep [2]. Always answer 4 mL unless the question says "modified" or "current". [2]
- The 8 h is counted from the **time of burn**, not from arrival at hospital. [2]
- Fluid starts above 15% TBSA in adults and 10% in children in most texts; some keys say 20% in adults; choose 15% when both are not options. [2]
- Child head/legs: infant head 18%, leg 14% each; the adult rule of nines is the key's default unless the stem says child. [2]
- Pulse oximetry is falsely normal in CO poisoning, a favoured trap. [2]
- Colloid: classic Parkland gives none in the first 24 h; Evans and Brooke include colloid. [2]
