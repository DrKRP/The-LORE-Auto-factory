# Trauma primary survey (ATLS)

> The **primary survey** is the rapid, prioritised assessment of an injured patient in the sequence **ABCDE**, in which each life-threatening problem is treated as soon as it is found, before moving on [1]. It is the core of the **Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS)** course of the American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma, and it is followed by resuscitation, the secondary survey and definitive care [1].

## High-yield
- Primary survey order: **A**irway with restriction of cervical spine motion, **B**reathing and ventilation, **C**irculation with haemorrhage control, **D**isability (neurological status), **E**xposure and environmental control [1].
- The order follows the principle **"treat the greatest threat to life first"**: loss of airway kills faster than loss of breathing, which kills faster than loss of circulating blood volume [1].
- A patient who **talks normally** has, for the moment, a patent airway, adequate breathing and adequate cerebral perfusion [1].
- A **GCS of 8 or less** is an indication for a **definitive airway** [1].
- **Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis**; treat it with immediate decompression and **do not wait for a chest X-ray** [1].
- **Haemorrhage** is the leading cause of preventable death after injury, and hypotension in a trauma patient is treated as **hypovolaemic (haemorrhagic) shock until proven otherwise** [1].
- In haemorrhagic shock, **hypotension first appears in Class III** (31–40% blood volume loss) [1].
- Initial fluid bolus in ATLS 10th edition: **1 L warmed isotonic crystalloid in adults, 20 mL/kg in children** [1].
- **Surgical cricothyroidotomy** is the preferred emergency surgical airway in adults and is avoided in children under about 12 years, in whom **needle cricothyroidotomy** is used [1].
- The **secondary survey** (head-to-toe examination with **AMPLE** history) starts only after the primary survey is complete and resuscitation is under way [1].

## Definition
- **ATLS** is a standardised training programme for the initial management of trauma patients, developed by the **American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma (ACS COT)** [1].
- The course originated in **Nebraska, USA (1978)** after the orthopaedic surgeon **James Styner**'s family was injured in a 1976 plane crash and received inadequate rural care [1].
- The **primary survey** is the first, rapid assessment (ABCDE) that identifies and simultaneously treats immediately life-threatening conditions [1].
- The **"golden hour"** is the early period after injury during which prompt resuscitation most improves survival; ATLS is aimed at deaths occurring in this period [1].
- ATLS sequence of initial assessment: preparation → triage → **primary survey (ABCDE)** with simultaneous resuscitation → adjuncts to primary survey → consider need for patient transfer → **secondary survey** → adjuncts to secondary survey → continued post-resuscitation monitoring and re-evaluation → definitive care [1].

## Causes / Risk factors
- **Trimodal distribution of trauma deaths** (Trunkey) [1]:
  - **First peak**: seconds to minutes after injury (e.g. severe brain or high spinal cord injury, rupture of heart or aorta); reduced only by **injury prevention** [1].
  - **Second peak**: minutes to several hours after injury (e.g. subdural/epidural haematoma, haemopneumothorax, ruptured spleen, liver laceration, pelvic fracture, multiple injuries with major blood loss); this is the peak **ATLS targets** [1].
  - **Third peak**: days to weeks after injury, from **sepsis and multiple organ dysfunction** [1].
- Modern trauma-system data show a bimodal rather than trimodal pattern in many settings, as late deaths have fallen [1] ⚠ Unverified

## Pathophysiology
- Airway obstruction can be sudden and complete, insidious and partial, or progressive and recurrent [1].
- The most common cause of airway obstruction in an unconscious patient is the **tongue falling back** against the posterior pharynx [1].
- In tension pneumothorax, a one-way valve air leak traps air in the pleural space, collapsing the lung, shifting the mediastinum to the opposite side and **impeding venous return**, causing obstructive shock [1].
- An **open pneumothorax** ("sucking chest wound") causes impaired ventilation when the chest wall defect is about **two-thirds of the tracheal diameter or larger**, because air then passes preferentially through the defect [1].
- In **flail chest**, the main cause of hypoxia is the **underlying pulmonary contusion**, not the paradoxical chest-wall movement itself [1].
- The **lethal triad** of trauma is **hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy**, each of which worsens the others [1].
- Total blood volume is about **7% of body weight (≈70 mL/kg) in adults** and about **80 mL/kg in children** [1].
- **Neurogenic shock** from spinal cord injury causes hypotension **with bradycardia** (loss of sympathetic tone), unlike haemorrhagic shock, which causes tachycardia [1].
- Head injury alone rarely causes hypotension except in the terminal stage or in infants; a hypotensive head-injured patient should be searched for another source of blood loss [1].

## Clinical features
### Airway (A)
- Signs of airway compromise: agitation, obtundation, cyanosis, use of accessory muscles, **noisy breathing (snoring, gurgling, stridor)**, hoarseness and tracheal deviation [1].
- Assume a **cervical spine injury** in every patient with multisystem blunt trauma, especially with altered consciousness or blunt injury above the clavicle [1].

### Breathing (B)
- Life-threatening chest injuries to identify in the primary survey: **tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive haemothorax, flail chest with pulmonary contusion, and cardiac tamponade** (the last assessed with circulation) [1].
- **Tension pneumothorax**: respiratory distress, tachycardia, hypotension, **tracheal deviation away** from the affected side, unilateral absent breath sounds, **hyper-resonance**, distended neck veins and, late, cyanosis [1].
- **Massive haemothorax**: shock with absent breath sounds and **dullness to percussion** on one side; neck veins may be flat from hypovolaemia [1].
- **Flail chest**: two or more adjacent ribs each fractured in **two or more places**, producing a segment with **paradoxical movement** [1].

### Circulation (C)
- Key bedside indicators of perfusion: **level of consciousness, skin colour, and pulse** (rate and character) [1].
- Sites of major occult blood loss — **"blood on the floor and four more"**: external bleeding, **chest, abdomen/retroperitoneum, pelvis and long bones** (especially femur) [1].
- **Cardiac tamponade**: **Beck's triad** = hypotension, **muffled heart sounds** and **distended neck veins** [1].
- Classes of haemorrhagic shock (70-kg adult) [1]:

| Class | Blood loss | Heart rate | Systolic BP | Pulse pressure | Resp. rate | Urine output (mL/h) | Mental state |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | <15% (<750 mL) | <100 / normal | Normal | Normal or ↑ | 14–20 | >30 | Slightly anxious |
| II | 15–30% (750–1500 mL) | 100–120 | Normal | **↓** | 20–30 | 20–30 | Mildly anxious |
| III | 31–40% (1500–2000 mL) | 120–140 | **↓** | ↓ | 30–40 | 5–15 | Anxious, confused |
| IV | >40% (>2000 mL) | >140 | ↓↓ | ↓ | >35 | Negligible | Confused, lethargic |

- **Narrowed pulse pressure** (Class II) is an early sign of haemorrhage, appearing before systolic BP falls [1].
- ATLS 10th edition adds **base deficit** to the classification: about 0 to −2 (I), −2 to −6 (II), −6 to −10 (III), −10 or less (IV) [1] ⚠ Unverified
- Tachycardia may be absent in the elderly, athletes, patients on **beta-blockers** and those with pacemakers [1].
- In children, **hypotension is a late sign** of shock because of their large physiological reserve [1].

### Disability (D)
- Rapid neurological evaluation: **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)**, **pupil size and reaction**, lateralising signs and level of spinal cord injury [1].
- **AVPU** (Alert, responds to Voice, responds to Pain, Unresponsive) is a quick alternative grading of consciousness [1].
- A decreased level of consciousness should prompt re-evaluation of oxygenation, ventilation and perfusion; hypoglycaemia, alcohol and drugs are considered only after these are excluded [1].

### Exposure (E)
- The patient is completely undressed and **log-rolled** to examine the back, then covered with warm blankets to prevent **hypothermia** [1].

## Investigations
### Adjuncts to the primary survey
- **ECG monitoring**, **pulse oximetry**, **ventilatory rate**, **arterial blood gas** and **end-tidal CO2 (capnography)** [1].
- **Urinary catheter** to monitor urine output; it is **contraindicated** when urethral injury is suspected — **blood at the urethral meatus**, perineal ecchymosis or a **high-riding prostate** — until a **retrograde urethrogram** confirms an intact urethra [1].
- **Gastric tube** to decompress the stomach; insert it **orally (orogastric)** when a **cribriform plate (anterior skull base) fracture** is suspected [1].
- **Chest X-ray (AP)** and **pelvic X-ray (AP)** in blunt trauma [1].
- **FAST** (Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma) looks for free fluid in four windows: **pericardial (subxiphoid), right upper quadrant (hepatorenal / Morison's pouch), left upper quadrant (splenorenal) and pelvis (suprapubic)** [1].
- **eFAST** (extended FAST) adds views of both pleural spaces to detect pneumothorax and haemothorax [1].
- **Diagnostic peritoneal lavage (DPL)** is the alternative bedside test for intra-abdominal bleeding in the haemodynamically abnormal patient; it has been largely replaced by FAST [1].
- Imaging and adjuncts must **not delay resuscitation** or transfer; a haemodynamically unstable patient should not go to the CT scanner [1].
- Urine output targets: **0.5 mL/kg/h in adults**, **1 mL/kg/h in children**, and **2 mL/kg/h in infants under 1 year** [1].

## Management
### A — Airway with restriction of cervical spine motion
- Basic manoeuvres: **chin lift** or **jaw thrust** (jaw thrust preferred when cervical spine injury is possible), suction, and **oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway** [1].
- An oropharyngeal airway is used only in an **unconscious** patient without a gag reflex [1].
- A **nasopharyngeal airway** is avoided when a **basal skull (cribriform plate) fracture** is suspected [1].
- During airway manipulation the head and neck are kept neutral with **manual in-line stabilisation**; a cervical collar alone is not sufficient during intubation [1].
- ATLS 10th edition uses the term **"spinal motion restriction"** in place of "spinal immobilisation" [1].
- A **definitive airway** is a tube placed **in the trachea with the cuff inflated below the vocal cords**, connected to oxygen-enriched assisted ventilation and secured in place [1].
- Definitive airways are **orotracheal tube, nasotracheal tube, and surgical airway** (cricothyroidotomy or tracheostomy) [1].
- Indications for a definitive airway: inability to maintain or protect the airway (e.g. **GCS ≤8**, severe maxillofacial fractures, risk of aspiration, risk of obstruction from neck haematoma, laryngeal or tracheal injury, **inhalation burns**), inability to maintain oxygenation or ventilation, and apnoea [1].
- **Orotracheal intubation with rapid-sequence induction** and in-line stabilisation is the usual method [1].
- When intubation fails and the airway cannot be maintained, a **surgical airway** is indicated; **surgical cricothyroidotomy** is preferred to emergency tracheostomy because it is faster, easier and bleeds less [1].
- **Needle cricothyroidotomy** with jet insufflation is a temporary measure; adequate oxygenation lasts only about **30–45 minutes** because CO2 accumulates [1].
- Surgical cricothyroidotomy is not recommended in children **under 12 years** because of the risk of damage to the **cricoid cartilage**, the only circumferential support of the upper trachea [1].
- In suspected **laryngeal fracture**, emergency **tracheostomy** rather than cricothyroidotomy is indicated when intubation is not possible [1] ⚠ Unverified

### B — Breathing and ventilation
- Every injured patient receives **supplemental oxygen** [1].
- **Tension pneumothorax**: immediate **needle decompression** followed by **tube thoracostomy** [1].
- ATLS 10th edition site for needle decompression in **adults**: **4th or 5th intercostal space just anterior to the mid-axillary line** [1].
- ATLS 10th edition site for needle decompression in **children**: **2nd intercostal space in the mid-clavicular line** [1].
- **Finger thoracostomy** is an acceptable alternative to needle decompression [1].
- **Open pneumothorax**: cover with a sterile occlusive dressing **taped on three sides** (flutter-valve effect), then place a chest tube **remote from the wound** [1].
- **Chest tube** is inserted in the **5th intercostal space, just anterior to the mid-axillary line**, within the "**triangle of safety**" [1].
- ATLS 10th edition recommends a chest tube of about **28–32 French** for haemothorax [1] ⚠ Unverified
- **Massive haemothorax** = rapid accumulation of **>1500 mL** of blood (or about one-third of blood volume) in the chest; treat with chest tube and volume replacement [1].
- Indications for **thoracotomy** in haemothorax: **>1500 mL drained immediately**, or **200 mL/h for 2–4 hours**, or ongoing need for transfusion [1].
- **Flail chest with pulmonary contusion**: oxygen, adequate **analgesia**, judicious fluids, and ventilation (intubation) if hypoxia persists [1].

### C — Circulation with haemorrhage control
- **External haemorrhage** is controlled by **direct manual pressure**; a **tourniquet** is used for life-threatening limb bleeding not controlled by pressure [1].
- Venous access: **two large-calibre (at least 18-gauge) peripheral IV catheters** [1].
- If peripheral access fails, use **intraosseous** access or a central/venous cut-down line [1].
- Draw blood for **type and crossmatch**, baseline labs and **pregnancy test** in females of childbearing age at cannulation [1].
- Fluids are **warmed to about 39 °C** to prevent hypothermia [1].
- Initial fluid in ATLS 10th edition: **1 L isotonic crystalloid in adults, 20 mL/kg in children under 40 kg**, then **blood products** early [1].
- **Balanced resuscitation / permissive hypotension**: accept a lower-than-normal BP until bleeding is controlled to avoid "popping the clot", **not** used in traumatic brain injury [1].
- **Response to initial fluid** guides further management: **rapid responders**, **transient responders** (ongoing bleeding — need blood and usually surgery) and **minimal/non-responders** (need immediate surgery or angioembolisation) [1].
- **Massive transfusion** is classically defined as **>10 units of packed red cells in 24 hours** [1].
- Massive transfusion protocols give plasma, platelets and red cells in a **balanced ratio approaching 1:1:1** [1].
- **Tranexamic acid** is given **within 3 hours of injury**: **1 g IV over 10 minutes, then 1 g over 8 hours** (CRASH-2) [1].
- **O-negative** blood is used when uncrossmatched blood is needed in females of childbearing age; type-specific blood is used when available [1].
- **Pelvic fracture** bleeding: apply a **pelvic binder (or sheet) centred over the greater trochanters** [1].
- Vasopressors are **contraindicated as first-line treatment** of haemorrhagic shock [1].
- **Cardiac tamponade**: **pericardiocentesis** is a temporising measure; definitive treatment is surgical (thoracotomy / pericardial window) [1].

### D — Disability
- Prevent **secondary brain injury** by maintaining adequate **oxygenation and perfusion** [1].
- Early neurosurgical consultation for a patient with a declining GCS or lateralising signs [1].

### E — Exposure / environmental control
- Prevent hypothermia: **warm room, warm blankets, warmed IV fluids and blood**, and external warming devices [1].

### Throughout
- **Reassess the primary survey (ABCDE) whenever the patient deteriorates** [1].
- If the patient needs a higher level of care, arrange **transfer early**; do not delay it for diagnostic tests [1].
- **Secondary survey**: complete head-to-toe examination and **AMPLE** history — **A**llergies, **M**edications, **P**ast illnesses / **P**regnancy, **L**ast meal, **E**vents / **E**nvironment related to injury [1].
- **Tertiary survey**: repeat complete examination, usually within **24 hours**, to find missed injuries [1].

### Special populations
- **Pregnancy**: the best initial treatment for the fetus is **optimal resuscitation of the mother** [1].
- **Pregnancy** (after about 20 weeks): **manual left displacement of the uterus** or left lateral tilt (log-roll board ~15°) to relieve **aortocaval compression** [1].
- Pregnant patients can lose **up to about 30–35% of blood volume** before showing signs of shock, because of physiological hypervolaemia [1].
- **Elderly**: limited physiological reserve; normal vital signs may hide shock [1].

### Triage
- In a mass-casualty incident the **triage** colour code is **red = immediate**, **yellow = delayed**, **green = minor ("walking wounded")**, **black = dead/expectant** [1].
- When casualties do not exceed the facility's capacity, patients with **life-threatening problems and multiple injuries are treated first**; when they exceed it, those with the **greatest chance of survival** using the least time and resources are treated first [1].

## Complications
- Missed injuries, commonly found later on secondary or tertiary survey [1].
- Iatrogenic hypothermia, dilutional coagulopathy and **abdominal compartment syndrome** from excessive crystalloid infusion [1].
- Needle decompression may fail when the catheter is **too short to reach the pleural space** in a thick chest wall, which is why ATLS moved the adult site to the 4th/5th ICS [1].
- Cricothyroidotomy complications: haemorrhage, creation of a false passage, aspiration, laryngeal stenosis and vocal cord damage [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- **Needle decompression site**: PYQs and older texts have expected the **2nd intercostal space, mid-clavicular line**; ATLS 10th edition places it at the **4th/5th ICS just anterior to the mid-axillary line in adults** and keeps the 2nd ICS MCL for children [2][1].
- **Initial fluid volume**: older ATLS editions and PYQ keys expected **2 L crystalloid in adults**; ATLS 10th edition reduced it to **1 L**, with early blood products [2][1].
- **Fluid choice**: PYQs have expected **Ringer lactate** as the initial fluid; ATLS accepts warmed isotonic crystalloid (Ringer lactate or normal saline) and emphasises early blood [2][1].
- **"Golden hour"** and the **trimodal death distribution** remain classic exam answers, although newer data show fewer late deaths [2][1] ⚠ Unverified
- **Pulse-site BP estimates** (palpable radial ≈ 80, femoral ≈ 70, carotid ≈ 60 mmHg systolic) appear in older questions; they overestimate BP and are no longer taught by ATLS [2][1] ⚠ Unverified
- **C before A (xABCDE / <C>ABCDE)**: military and prehospital protocols (e.g. PHTLS, MARCH) put **catastrophic external haemorrhage control first**; for NEET PG the expected ATLS order remains **ABCDE**, with haemorrhage control inside C [2][1] ⚠ Unverified
- **Chest tube size**: older texts gave **36–40 Fr**; ATLS 10th edition suggests **28–32 Fr** for haemothorax [2][1] ⚠ Unverified
- **Cervical spine**: older answers say "immobilise with a hard collar"; ATLS 10th edition uses "**spinal motion restriction**" and manual in-line stabilisation during airway manoeuvres [2][1].
- The **"talking patient"** question: a patient who answers clearly has a patent airway for now — the next step is **B** (breathing), not intubation [2][1].
- Trap: in an unstable patient, **CT abdomen is wrong**; the expected answer is **FAST** (or DPL) in the resuscitation room [2][1].
