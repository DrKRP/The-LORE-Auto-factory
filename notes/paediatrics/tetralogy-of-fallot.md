# Tetralogy of Fallot

> Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is the **most common cyanotic congenital heart disease** [1][2]. Its clinical presentation varies with the severity of **right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) obstruction** [1].

## High-yield
- TOF is the most common cyanotic congenital heart disease in children **beyond the neonatal period** [3] ⚠ Unverified
- Classic TOF has four components: a **large, anteriorly malaligned VSD**, an **overriding aorta**, **infundibular (sub-pulmonary) RVOT obstruction** and **right ventricular hypertrophy** [1].
- Chest radiographs may classically show a **"boot-shaped" heart**, with an upturned apex due to right ventricular hypertrophy [4][5].
- **Pentalogy of Fallot** = TOF plus an **atrial septal defect**; the term is old and seldom used now [6].
- The **echocardiogram** is the gold standard for diagnosis and remains the primary diagnostic modality [1][4].
- The **22q11.2 deletion** is the most common syndromic association of TOF [2] ⚠ Unverified

## Definition
- Classic TOF is a congenital heart defect comprised of 4 anatomical alterations [1]:
  - A large, **anteriorly malaligned ventricular septal defect (VSD)** [1].
  - An **overriding aorta** [1].
  - **Infundibular (sub-pulmonary) RVOT obstruction** [1].
  - **Right ventricular hypertrophy**, secondary to chronic systemic pressures [1].
- **ASD and PDA** are among the other anomalies that *may accompany* TOF; the four components listed by StatPearls and CDC include no ASD, PDA or LVH [1][7].

## Causes / Risk factors
- StatPearls gives an incidence of 5 to 7 per 10,000 live births and a prevalence of 1 in 3,000 births [1].
- Garagiola and Thorne give an incidence of 3–5 per 10,000 live births, or 7–10% of all birth defects [3] ⚠ Unverified
- Gagliardi et al. give a worldwide birth prevalence of about 3 to 6 per 10,000 live births, about 7–10% of all congenital heart defects [2] ⚠ Unverified
- The **22q11.2 deletion** is the most common syndromic association, present in up to 15% of TOF cases [2] ⚠ Unverified
- In one TOF cohort, 22q11.2 deletion syndrome had a prevalence of 6% [3] ⚠ Unverified
- A **right aortic arch** is seen in 20–25% of TOF [1].

## Pathophysiology
- With significant RVOT obstruction, most of the blood shunts toward the aorta, bypassing the pulmonary bed [1].
- Chest radiographs can therefore show decreased pulmonary vascular markings (**pulmonary oligaemia**) [4][5].
- If the resistance across the RVOT is less than the systemic vascular resistance, the patient is minimally cyanotic or acyanotic [1].
- Infants with moderate RVOT obstruction who were not diagnosed before birth may be asymptomatic apart from a loud murmur, and may be referred to as **"pink Tets"** [1].

## Clinical features
- TOF most commonly presents as a **cyanotic neonate** [1].
- The murmur has a **harsh systolic ejection** quality, best heard at the left mid to upper sternal border with posterior radiation [1].
- The greater the degree of obstruction, the more prominent the murmur [1].
- The pulmonic component of **S2** is usually inaudible [1].
- **Tet (hypercyanotic) spells** can be triggered by agitation, pain, anaemia, fever, hypovolaemia or peripheral vasodilatation [1].
- During a tet spell there is a **decrease in the intensity of the murmur** [1].

## Investigations
- **ECG**: right axis deviation, prominent R or qR waves in V1 and upright T waves in V1, characteristic of right atrial enlargement and right ventricular hypertrophy [1].
- **Chest X-ray**: StatPearls describes a normal-size heart silhouette with an upturned apex and a concave or "boot-shaped" main pulmonary artery segment [1].
- The upturned apex of the **boot-shaped heart** is attributed to right ventricular hypertrophy, together with a concave pulmonary arterial segment [4][5].
- Most infants with TOF, however, may not show the boot-shaped finding [4] ⚠ Unverified
- **Echocardiogram**: the gold standard for the diagnosis of TOF [1].
- Echocardiography allows direct visualisation of the abnormal anatomy and remains the primary modality for diagnosis [4][5].
- Echocardiography has limitations in assessing associated extracardiac anomalies, such as peripheral pulmonary stenosis and atresia [4] ⚠ Unverified
- **Contrast-enhanced CT** is useful in demonstrating the complex cardiovascular morphology of TOF [4][5].
- CT can evaluate post-surgical changes, such as patency of palliative shunts, and complications [4] ⚠ Unverified
- **MRI** provides both anatomical detail and functional information without ionising radiation [4][5].
- Detailed pulmonary artery assessment matters because repairing the cardiac defects without addressing pulmonary artery hypoplasia or stenosis has a poor outcome [4] ⚠ Unverified
- The main or right pulmonary artery diameter should be compared with that of the ascending aorta [4] ⚠ Unverified

## Management
**Tet spell**
- Tet spells require a rapid and aggressive approach [1].
- **Knee-chest positioning**, to increase systemic vascular resistance [1].
- **Oxygen**, to cause pulmonary vasodilation [1].
- **IV fluid boluses**, to improve RV filling [1].
- **IV beta-blockers**, to decrease heart rate and aid RV filling further [1].
- **IV phenylephrine**, to increase systemic afterload [1].
- **Morphine** can be given as a sedative during a tet spell [1].

**Timing of complete repair**
- The timing of TOF repair is still in debate [1].
- StatPearls describes deferring complete surgical repair to after the first 3 months of life [1].
- Al Mosa et al. consider **3–6 months of age** likely the ideal time frame for elective repair, and advocate repair at 3–6 months in asymptomatic patients [8] ⚠ Unverified
- The same authors prefer primary neonatal or early repair for symptomatic infants with TOF [8] ⚠ Unverified

**Palliation (Blalock-Taussig shunt)**
- The first surgical treatment of TOF was a palliative subclavian-to-pulmonary-artery shunt by Blalock and Taussig [8] ⚠ Unverified
- The **Blalock-Taussig shunt** connects the subclavian artery to the ipsilateral branch pulmonary artery [1][8].
- Al Mosa et al. reserve staging with initial palliation for infants with high-risk features: low birth weight or prematurity, and small or discontinuous pulmonary arteries [8] ⚠ Unverified
- They do not palliate patients who present as neonates without high-risk features [8] ⚠ Unverified
- Drawbacks include the limited lifespan of the shunt and an increased incidence of late sudden death [8] ⚠ Unverified
- Morbidities of initial palliation include pulmonary artery stenosis and distortion, and smaller pulmonary arteries before repair [8] ⚠ Unverified

**After repair**
- Pulmonary valve replacement can be achieved surgically or by a transcatheter approach [1].

## Complications
**After repair**
- **Pulmonary regurgitation** results from reconstruction of the RVOT [1].
- **Pulmonary insufficiency** is the most common indication for reoperation [1].
- Arrhythmias can occur after TOF repair, including **ventricular tachycardia** [1].
- 40–50% of adults with repaired TOF can have ventricular arrhythmias [1].
- The risk of sudden death is 6–9% 30 years after the surgical procedure [1].

**Unrepaired TOF**
- The most frequent causes of mortality without surgical intervention include **hypoxic spells (68%)** [1].
- Cerebrovascular accidents (17%) and brain abscesses (13%) are also listed among the causes of mortality without surgery [1].
- Chronic hypoxaemia can cause hyperviscosity, abnormal haemostasis, stroke, **cerebral abscess** and endocarditis [1].
- Patients with TOF, repaired or unrepaired, are at risk of **endocarditis** [1].

## ⚠ Exam notes
- PYQs have expected TOF as the most common cyanotic CHD beyond the neonatal period, as Garagiola and Thorne state [3] ⚠ Unverified; current StatPearls says TOF most commonly presents as a cyanotic neonate [1].
- PYQs have expected pentalogy of Fallot = TOF + atrial septal defect; CDC confirms this and calls it an old term seldom used now [6].
- PYQs have keyed PDA, ASD and LVH as "not part of TOF"; current StatPearls lists the four components as a VSD, an overriding aorta, RVOT obstruction and right ventricular hypertrophy [1].
- PYQs have keyed the boot-shaped heart to TOF; Radiopaedia says most infants with TOF may not show this finding [4] ⚠ Unverified
- Sources differ on 22q11.2 deletion frequency: up to 15% of TOF cases [2] versus 6% in one TOF cohort [3].
- Sources differ on frequency: 5 to 7 per 10,000 live births in StatPearls [1], 3–5 per 10,000 in Garagiola and Thorne [3], and about 3 to 6 per 10,000 in Gagliardi et al. [2].

## Unverified claims
Each claim below is quoted correctly from its source, but rests on a single tier-2 source. The rescue search found no tier-1 page or independent second source.
- "Beyond the neonatal period" qualifier on most common cyanotic CHD (Garagiola and Thorne only; StatPearls and CDC say only "most common cyanotic").
- 22q11.2 deletion as *the most common* syndromic association, and its 15% / 6% frequencies (StatPearls lists it without ranking it).
- Incidence figures 3–5 and 3–6 per 10,000 (Garagiola and Thorne; Gagliardi et al.).
- "Most infants may not show the boot-shaped heart" (Radiopaedia only).
- Echo limitations, CT for shunt patency, pulmonary artery assessment and comparison with the aorta (Radiopaedia only).
- 3–6 month repair timing, early repair if symptomatic, BT shunt history, palliation indications, drawbacks and morbidities (Al Mosa et al. only).
